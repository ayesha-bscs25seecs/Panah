# **PART I — A PRACTICAL GUIDE TO ISLAMIC LAWS OF INHERITANCE** 

_By Muhammad Ahsan Zafar. Dedicated to Eng. Malik Bashir Ahmad Baghvi. Material is for teaching/learning purposes; contact_ _<u>AHSANZZ@GMAIL.COM</u> for permissions or certification testing._ 

## **1. Terminologies** 

- **Mirath (Gross Inheritance):** All movable or immovable property left behind by the deceased, whether earned, inherited, or gifted. 

- **Warith (Heir):** A relative who may potentially inherit from the deceased's wealth. 

- **Wassiya (Will):** An order allocating a portion of the deceased's property, executed only after death. A will can only cover up to **1/3 of the estate** ; any excess is capped at 1/3. A will **cannot** be made in favor of a person who is already a legal heir (warith). 

- **Walad / Awlaad (Children, "CH"):** Son (S), daughter (D), son's son (SS), son's daughter (SD), son's son's son (SSS), son's son's daughter (SSD). Other grandchildren (e.g., daughter's children) are _not_ included in "CH" and fall under Dhil-Irham instead. 

- **Ikhwa (Siblings, "IK"):** Two or more real, paternal, or maternal brothers/sisters, regardless of type or gender. 

   - **Real brother/sister (RB/RS):** Same parents as deceased ( _haqiqi_ ). 

   - **Paternal brother/sister (PB/PS):** mother ( _allati_ ). 

   - **Maternal brother/sister (MT):** father ( _akhyafi_ ). 

- **Dhil-Furoodh:** ("sharers"). 

- **Asbah (Residuary, "Res"):** 

take whatever remains after Dhil-Furoodh are paid. Generally male relatives connected through a chain of males (exceptions: real sister and paternal sister can be residuaries in some configurations). 

- **Dhil-Irham (DI):** 3rd-tier heirs, entitled only if there is leftover property and no Asbah exists. The closest relative in this category takes everything remaining. 

- **Kalala:** A person who, at death, has no living children (S, D, SS, SD, SSS, SSD) and no living father or grandfather.

## **3. Steps of Wealth Distribution (Sequence)** 

1. **Funeral expenditure** (modest) 

2. **Payment of debts / loans** 

3. **Pending commitments** 

4. **Will** — up to 1/3 of what remains 

5. **Distribution among heirs** as prescribed 

## **4. Calculating Shares — Step-by-Step Method** 

**Step 1:** Write the deceased's name, date of death, and distributable wealth (the amount remaining after steps 1–4 of wealth distribution above). 

**Step 2:** List all relatives alive at time of death. Mark any relative who qualifies for extended Residuary (Asbah) status with "R" (male relatives connected via a chain of males). 

**Step 3a:** Use Table 1 to assign shares to each applicable heir based on conditions. 

**Step 3b:** For these 5 relation-pairs, if both the male and female version are present, ignore the female's Table 1 share (mark with ") and extract her share from Table 2 instead: 

1. Son (S) & Daughter (D) 

2. Son's son (SS) & Son's daughter (SD) 

3. Son's son's son (SSS) & Son's son's daughter (SSD) 

4. Real brother (RB) & Real sister (RS) 

5. Paternal brother (PB) & Paternal sister (PS) 

**Step 4:** Find the LCM (lowest common multiple) of the denominators of assigned shares (can be skipped if using the "Share from 24" column directly — denominator is always 24 or less). Example: shares of 1/6, 1/2, 1/8 → LCM = 24 → numerators become 4, 12, 3 out of 24. 

**Step 5:** Calculate remaining shares = common denominator − sum of assigned numerators. Example: 24 − (4+12+3) = 5 remaining shares. 

**Step 6:** Move left→right through Table 2. Assign all remaining shares to the first applicable category. If that category is one of the 5 relation-pairs from Step 3b, males get 2x the share of females (multiply male count ×2, add female count, divide shares by that total, give 2 portions per male and 1 per female). 

- Example: 2 Sons + 2 Daughters assigned 12 shares → (2×2)+2 = 6 → 12/6 = 2 shares per unit → each son gets 4, each daughter gets 2. 

**Step 6b:** If shares are assigned to the extended Residuary (Asbah) category via Table 2, use **Table 3** to find the nearest qualifying Asbah relative. 

**Step 7:** Divide the shares in each category by the number of individuals in that category to get each person's portion. If division produces a decimal, multiply the entire column by the needed factor to keep whole numbers (e.g., 13 shares ÷ 6 people → multiply by 6 → 78 total shares, 13 each). 

**Step 8:** Convert shares into wealth terms using: 

**(Share of the person ÷ Total shares) × Total wealth = Wealth share of each person** 

**Two exceptions to watch for:** Problem of Excess ( _Masla-Radd_ ) and Problem of Deficiency ( _Masla-Aul_ ) — see Section 6 below.

## **10. Ineligibility for Inheritance** 

1. **Homicide:** inheriting from them, even with a prescribed share. _"One who kills a man cannot inherit from him."_ (Tirmidhi, Ibn Majah, Abu Dawud) 

2. A non-Muslim cannot inherit from a Muslim, and vice versa. A will (up to 1/3) can be used to provide for a nonMuslim relative instead. _"A Muslim does not inherit from a disbeliever, a disbeliever does not inherit from a Muslim."_ (Sahih Bukhari & Muslim, Book of Fara'id) 

3. **Illegitimate Child:** A child born out of wedlock is legally regarded only as the child of the mother. Falsely denying paternity is considered a major sin. (Reference: case of _Lian_ during the Prophet's lifetime, Sahih Bukhari, Book of Fara'id) 

_rules apply to individual cases._

## **12. Making a Will (Wassiya)** 

- **When to write it:** As soon as possible, while of sound mind. _"It is not permissible for any Muslim who has something to will to stay for two nights without having his last will and testament written and kept ready with him."_ (Prophet Muhammad  ,Sahih ملسو هيلع هللا ىلص 

- Bukhari) 

- **How much can be willed:** Up to **1/3 (33.3%)** of wealth, maximum. Referenced hadith: Saad bin Abi Waqqas (RA) was told by the Prophet  that even 1/3 "is too much," and it is better to leave ملسو هيلع هللا ىلص 

- heirs wealthy than poor and begging. Ibn Abbas (RA) recommended reducing the bequest further to 1/4. 

- **Rules:** A will **cannot** be made for someone who is already a legal heir with a fixed share, nor can it deny any heir their due share. It 

**can** be used for people who don't otherwise inherit (orphaned grandchild, non-Muslim relative, adopted child, stepchild/parent) or for charity. _"Allah has appointed for everyone who has a right what is due to him, and no bequest must be made to an heir."_ (Abu Dawud & Ibn Majah) 

- **When applicable:** Only after death. Can be revised repeatedly during the person's lifetime; the final version governs. 

- **Consequences of a wrong will** (e.g., exceeding 1/3, willing to an existing heir, or denying a legitimate heir's share): considered a major sin; should be corrected during the person's life, or by heirs after death. _"A man may do good deeds for 70 years but if he acts unjustly when he leaves his last testament... he will enter the Fire..."_ (Musnad Ahmad & Ibn Majah) 

- **Practical requirements for a legitimate will:** 

   - Written or verbal, witnessed by two honest believers with no 

   - Must also comply with local law for legal execution. Example: in the USA, a will must be written and signed by two disinterested witnesses; U.S. law permits Islamic-lawbased distribution if explicitly stated in the will — otherwise default state/country rules apply.

## **3. Inheritance (Mirath / Faraid)** 

### **3.1 The Islamic Basis — Quranic Shares** 

Faraid ("the obligatory shares") derives primarily from **Surah An-Nisa (4), verses 11, 12, and 176** , which fix exact fractional shares for specific relatives ("Quranic heirs"/"sharers") before anyone else inherits. 

- **Quran 4:11** (children & parents): son's share = 2× daughter's share; 2+ daughters with no son jointly take 2/3; a single daughter with no son takes 1/2; each parent takes 1/6 if deceased left children; if no children and only parents inherit, mother takes 1/3 (1/6 if there are siblings) — all after bequests/debts.¹ 

- **Quran 4:12** (spouses): husband inherits 1/2 (no children) or 1/4 (with children); wife/wives collectively inherit 1/4 (no children) or 1/8 (with children) — after bequests/debts.² 

- **Quran 4:176** (siblings, kalalah case — no children/parents) sets siblings' shares.³ 

⁴ 

1. **Sharers (ashab al-furud)** wife, daughter, father, mother, and others (siblings, grandparents) depending on survivors. 

2. **Residuaries (asabah)** — mostly male agnatic relatives (sons, then further male-line relatives) splitting the remainder in fixed priority order. A son always inherits as residuary alongside daughters (daughters are sharers, son absorbs residue — this is how the "male gets 2x female" rule in 4:11 plays out). 

3. **Distant kindred (zawil arham)** — remote relatives (e.g., daughter's children, sister's children), inheriting only if no sharers/ residuaries exist. If no one in any tier survives, the estate escheats to the state. 

_Worked example: man dies leaving a widow and two sons, no other sharers — widow takes 1/8 (he had children); the two sons split the remaining 7/8 equally as residuaries._ ⁵ 

#### **numerically:** 

- Only **two classes** of heirs — sharers and residuaries — no separate "distant kindred" category (folded into class-based system).⁶ 

- Heirs divided into classes by blood/marriage proximity (parents & children; grandparents & siblings; uncles/aunts); a nearer class **fully excludes** a more remote one — unlike Sunni law where sharers and residuaries inherit simultaneously.⁷ 

- Distribution generally follows **per-stirpes** (by branch) logic in several situations, versus the Sunni per-capita approach — can change how much a specific grandchild or sibling's descendant receives.⁸ 

- Shia law allows **radd** (return of leftover residue to sharers, excluding spouses) more broadly, and does not apply the Sunni **aul** doctrine (proportional reduction when shares exceed the whole) the same way.⁹ 

**Product implication:** Cannot build one calculator and relabel it "Shia mode" by changing numbers — needs a separately validated calculation path, ideally reviewed by someone versed in Ithna Ashari fiqh (e.g., Lucy Carroll's comparative academic work is a strong starting citation, not a blog).¹⁰ 

### **3.2 The Pakistani Statutory Layer** 

- **West Pakistan Muslim Personal Law (Shariat) Application Act, 1962** — confirms that for Muslims, "intestate succession, special 

- property of females… marriage, dissolution of marriage… maintenance, dower, guardianship, gifts, trusts and trust properties, and wakfs" are governed by Muslim Personal Law rather than regional custom.¹¹ Deliberate correction: previously many Punjabi/tribal customary rules excluded women from land inheritance entirely. **Section 3** specifically terminates limited/life estates Muslim women held under customary law, replacing them with full ownership under actual Shariat rules.¹² 

- **MFLO 1961, Section 4 — orphaned grandchildren:** modification to classical Hanafi doctrine — if a son/daughter of the deceased predeceased them, that dead child's own children step into their parent's place and inherit per stirpes.¹³ Under strict classical Hanafi law, such grandchildren could be excluded entirely if an uncle/aunt is alive. **This provision has been actively contested** : the Federal Shariat Court has been asked whether Section 4 is compatible with Islamic injunctions, and appeals on this question have been pending before superior courts.¹⁴ **Do not present Section 4 as settled.** 

- **Non-Muslims:** The **Succession Act, 1925** governs substantive inheritance for Christians, Hindus, Parsis, and other non-Muslim Pakistanis, and also supplies general succession-certificate procedure (Sections 370–390) used across communities.¹⁵ 

### **3.3 Enforcement: Prevention of Anti-Women Practices Act, 2011** 

Arguably the single most important practical law for this space — the core problem is usually not that the law denies women inheritance (Islamic and statutory law both affirm it) but that families pressure, deceive, or forge documents to prevent women from claiming it. 

Amendments to the Pakistan Penal Code: 

- **Section 498-A** inheritance by deceitful/illegal means; punishable by **5–10 years'** 

#### .¹⁶ 

- **Section 498-B** — criminalizes forced marriage (3–10 years plus fine).¹⁷ 

- **Section 498-C** — criminalizes "marriage to the Quran" (coercing a woman to swear on the Quran never to marry, to block her inheritance claim), and targets vani/swara (compensation marriages settling feuds/disputes).¹⁸ 

Independent research reports the Act remains **weakly enforced and not well known** , with ambiguous statutory language and low reporting rates due to family/social pressure.¹⁹ The agent should affirm the law exists and is on women's side, while being honest that enforcement is inconsistent.

## **4. Marriage Registration & Polygamy** 

- **Registration (MFLO s.5):** Every Muslim marriage must be registered with a licensed Nikah Registrar; solemnizing without registration reporting incurs fine/imprisonment. Registration protects a woman's ability to prove her marriage later — critical for maintenance, inheritance, and custody claims.²⁴ 

- **Polygamy (MFLO s.6):** A man may not contract a further marriage during an existing marriage without prior **written permission of an Arbitration Council** , which must assess whether the new marriage is "necessary and just" and whether existing wife/wives' consent was obtained. Marrying without permission requires him to **immediately pay the entire dower** (prompt and deferred) to the existing wife/wives (recoverable as arrears of land revenue), plus liability for up to one year's ⁵ Courts treat an unauthorized second 

- imprisonment and/or fine.² marriage as valid, freestanding grounds for the first wife to seek dissolution — in at least one cited case, the Supreme Court restored her full dower rather than requiring any return.²⁶ 

## **5. Divorce** 

Four broad legal routes to end a Muslim marriage in Pakistan, with differing financial consequences. 

### **5.1 Talaq (husband-initiated)** 

Governed by **MFLO Section 7** . A husband pronouncing talaq (any form) must give written notice to the Union Council Chairman, with a copy to the wife. Talaq does **not** take legal effect for **90 days** from the Chairman's receipt of notice (or until end of pregnancy, if later) — during 

which the Chairman must form an Arbitration Council to attempt reconciliation. Pronouncing talaq without this notice is an offence (up to 1 year imprisonment and/or fine) — but courts have held the talaq can still be religiously/legally effective even without notice, a real practical gap: an unregistered talaq is hard to prove or disprove later.²⁷ 

### **5.2 Talaq-e-tafweez (delegated divorce) & khula by agreement (MFLO s.8)** 

If the nikahnama delegates the right of talaq to the wife (very commonly a standard clause — **Column 18** of the Pakistani nikahnama form), she can exercise it herself, following essentially the same notice procedure as §7.²⁸ 

### **5.3 Judicial Divorce Sought by the Wife — Dissolution of Muslim Marriages Act, 1939** 

Lists the grounds on which a wife can go to Family Court and get judicial dissolution regardless of husband's agreement. **Section 2 grounds:**²⁹ 

1. Husband's whereabouts unknown for 4 years. 

2. Husband neglected/failed to provide maintenance for 2 years. 

3. Husband took an additional wife in violation of MFLO's polygamypermission rule. 

4. Husband sentenced to imprisonment of 7+ years. 

5. Husband failed, without reasonable cause, to perform marital obligations for 3 years. 

6. Husband impotent at time of marriage and remains so. 

7. Husband insane for 2 years, or has leprosy, or a virulent venereal disease. 

- repudiates the marriage before turning 18, if unconsummated. 

9. **Cruelty** even without physical violence; associating with women of ill repute or an infamous life; forcing her into an immoral life; disposing of her property or obstructing her legal rights over it; obstructing her religious practice; in polygamous marriages, 

inequitable treatment per the Quran. 

10. Any other ground recognized as valid under Muslim Law — the "catch-all" courts have used to expand recognized grounds (including, functionally, khula itself). 

**Section 4:** A Muslim woman's conversion away from Islam does **not** by itself dissolve her marriage — she must seek dissolution on one of the above grounds. **Section 5:** using this Act does not affect her right to dower — a separate, independently enforceable right.³⁰ 

### **5.4 Khula — the Wife's Right to Release Herself, and What It Costs Her** 

The word "khula" doesn't appear in the 1939 Act's text — it developed through case law reading Islamic khula doctrine into the "any other ground" clause. In practice, courts treat khula as the wife's near-absolute right: she need not prove fault or cruelty — generally enough that she states she cannot continue living within the limits prescribed by God (marriage broken down from her side); courts are bound to grant it.³¹ Husband's consent is not required.³² 

because khula is treated as release in exchange for consideration, courts have traditionally required her to **return the dower** already received (or forgo dower still owed) — the biggest thing women lose compared to dissolution on a proven ground (e.g., cruelty/ non-maintenance), where dower is not automatically forfeited.³³ ³⁴ 

#### **This area is live/unsettled, not settled — recent developments:** 

- **2022:** The Federal Shariat Court struck down subsections (5) and (6) of Family Courts Act 1964 §10, which had given family courts a fixed formula for dower on khula. This created a legal gap being filled case by case since.³⁵ 

- **2026 (Lahore High Court):** Ruled that a wife's dower right remains protected where the marriage was dissolved due to husband's cruelty/misconduct — even if the decree is formally labelled "khula." The court distinguished "khula simpliciter" (pure personal aversion, forfeiture logic still applies) from dissolution caused by husband's fault ("nashuz"), and called on the Punjab 

legislature to enact a clear replacement rule.³⁶ 

- **May 2026 (Supreme Court):** A three-member bench (Chief Justice Yahya Afridi; judgment authored by Justice Shahid Bilal Hassan) held that a family court **cannot automatically convert a cruelty case into a khula case without the wife's clear, informed, and voluntary consent** — because doing so can silently cost her financial rights (especially dower) she'd keep if her cruelty claim succeeded. Where cruelty isn't fully proven but the marriage has genuinely broken down, the wife must still be given the choice to proceed via khula rather than forced to keep litigating fault. The Court set a lower **"preponderance of probability"** evidentiary standard for domestic disputes (rejecting any requirement for eyewitnesses or FIRs), and adopted a broad cruelty definition including sustained mental/emotional abuse, not only physical violence.³⁷ 

**Bottom line for end users:** As of the May 2026 ruling, a woman does not have to choose between "proving cruelty" and "getting khula quickly" as a forced trade-off — courts must explain this choice and get her informed consent before treating her case as khula, because khula can cost her the dower she might otherwise keep. Flag this prominently. 

### **5.5 Lian** 

Where a husband accuses his wife of zina (adultery) and she denies it, she may seek dissolution on this ground (added to the DMMA by the Protection of Women (Criminal Laws Amendment) Act, 2006, as clause (viia)).³⁸ 

## **6. Mehr (Dower / Haq Mehr)** 

**What it is:** An obligatory payment/property the husband owes the wife as a term of the marriage contract itself — not a gift, not conditional on the marriage lasting, not something the family "keeps." Grounded in **Quran 4:4** ("give the women their due compensation"), recorded in the nikahnama.³⁹ Typically split into: 

- **Prompt dower (mu'ajjal)** — payable immediately/on demand. 

- **Deferred dower (mu'wajjal)** — payable on a triggering event, usually divorce or the husband's death.⁴⁰ 

**When owed, and to whom:** The Supreme Court has explicitly ruled that mehr must be paid **whenever the wife demands it** — not only owed upon divorce. If no timing was specified, she can demand it at any point during the marriage; the debt is enforceable directly against the husband.⁴ **MFLO** ¹ If mode/timing isn't specified in the nikahnama, **Section 10** presumes the entire amount is payable on demand.⁴² 

**Does she lose it if she leaves the marriage?** Genuinely factdependent as of mid-2026: 

- If dissolution is granted on a **proven statutory ground** under the DMMA (non-maintenance, cruelty, unauthorized second marriage) — courts have treated dower as **protected** , even where the decree paperwork says "khula."⁴³ 

- If she seeks release for her **own reasons, unrelated to husband's fault** ("khula simpliciter") — courts have traditionally directed her to return prompt dower already paid (or an agreed/ admitted portion) and treated unpaid deferred dower as forfeited — though not automatic anymore post-2022/2026; fact- and judge-dependent.⁴⁴ 

- If husband takes a **second wife without following MFLO permission procedure** , this is treated as his fault — a wife divorcing over this generally is not required to return dower; at least one reported Supreme Court case restored her full dower.⁴⁵ 

**Procedure:** Dower is recovered via a suit in Family Court under the Family Courts Act, 1964, regardless of how the marriage ended (talaq, khula, or judicial dissolution), so long as it hasn't already been paid or formally waived.⁴⁶ 

## **7. Maintenance (Nafaqa)** 

**During marriage:** A husband is legally obligated to maintain his wife (food, clothing, shelter, medical needs) regardless of her own income; 

her working does not reduce/excuse this obligation. If he fails, she can **MFLO Section 9** Arbitration seek a maintenance certificate via the Council process, and/or sue directly in Family Court. Generally survives even if she lives separately, provided she has a valid reason (cruelty, his failure to provide a home) — a wife found "disobedient" ( _nashiza_ ) without valid cause can lose this claim, a genuinely contested and gendered doctrine to present carefully.⁴⁷ ⁴⁸ 

**During iddat** (post-divorce waiting period): husband remains obligated to maintain her through iddat — **three menstrual cycles/three months** after divorce, or until childbirth if pregnant (Quran 65:4), or **four months and ten days** if a widow.⁴⁹ Pakistani case law: PLD 1981 Karachi 240 confirms maintenance is obligatory during iddat but not automatically beyond it "unless agreed"; PLD 2003 Lahore 15 confirmed a pregnant divorced wife's maintenance continues until childbirth regardless of iddat's normal length.⁵⁰ _(Note: these case citations are reported via secondary sources — verify against original PLD law reports before publishing to end users.)_ 

**After iddat:** A divorced wife is generally **not entitled to further maintenance for herself** once iddat ends, unless she has a separate agreement/contractual right — differs from some other Muslim-majority jurisdictions and broader statutory frameworks (e.g., India's CrPC) that extend it further; Pakistan's system does not currently do so as a matter of course.⁵¹ 

**Children's maintenance** is separate and does **not** end with the marriage. A father remains obligated to maintain his children until adulthood/financial independence, regardless of custody arrangements — even if the mother has custody (hizanat), the father must still pay child maintenance directly.⁵² 

## **8. Child Custody (Hizanat) & Guardianship (Wilayat)** 

- **Hizanat (custody):** day-to-day physical care and upbringing. 

- **Wilayat (guardianship):** for the child. 

Governed by the **Guardians and Wards Act, 1890** ; **Section 17** sets the overriding legal test: **the welfare of the minor** — not either parent's "right" — is paramount.⁵³ Islamic hizanat doctrine supplies the starting presumption, tested against actual child welfare: 

- **Mother's presumptive custody:** sons until ~age 7; daughters until puberty (Hanafi position).⁵⁴ 

- This presumption is **rebuttable** — the other parent can contest with evidence it wouldn't serve the child's welfare; courts do depart from it when warranted.⁵⁵ 

- court, she may lose the presumption.⁵⁶ 

- After the presumptive custody period ends, the father (as natural guardian) generally becomes entitled to physical custody too, again subject to welfare review — not automatic.⁵⁷ 

- **Custody is legally independent of how the marriage ended.** A mother who obtains khula does not lose custody rights by virtue of that alone; a father who pronounces talaq does not automatically gain custody.⁵⁸ 

- **Interim custody** can be granted under **Section 12** while a full custody case (a full trial, not a quick hearing) is pending; the parent without interim custody generally retains visitation rights.⁵⁹ 

- If both parents are unavailable, custody priority typically passes to other relatives (e.g., maternal/paternal grandmother), in an order recognized by fiqh and applied by courts.⁶⁰ 

**Procedure:** File a petition under the Guardians and Wards Act before the Guardian/Family Court where the child resides; other parent is served notice and can respond; courts often attempt reconciliation first; interim custody/visitation can be set while pending; final order turns on welfare factors (stability, education, child's own wishes at sufficient age, character of each parent, etc.); appeal lies to the High Court.⁶¹

**Q: Can my father or brothers legally stop me from inheriting?** No. Under Islamic law and the West Pakistan Muslim Personal Law (Shariat) Application Act, 1962, your inheritance share is a legal right, not something the family grants. Deliberately depriving you of it by deceit, pressure, or forged documents is a specific criminal offence under PPC §498-A (5–10 years' imprisonment and/or fine up to Rs. 1 million). (§3.3) 

**Q: Can I be made to sign away my inheritance?** A document signed under family pressure is not automatically binding; courts have held such waivers do not bar you from later claiming your actual share, except in narrow circumstances (e.g., a fully executed family settlement you knowingly benefited from). (§3.4) 

**Q: How much do I actually inherit as a daughter?** Depends who else survives (sons, parents, spouse, siblings) and your school of thought. General Hanafi Sunni rule: one daughter alone gets half the estate; two+ daughters together get two-thirds (before residuaries take the rest); if a son is also alive, daughters and sons split 1 female : 2 male shares of what's left after other sharers are paid. Shia calculation differs structurally, not just numerically — flag this and ask the user's sect before calculating. (§3.1) 

**Q: Do I lose my inheritance if I get divorced or remarry?** No — inheritance rights from your parents/family are unrelated to marital status. (Divorce affects rights against your ex-husband's estate specifically; dower is a separate right — see §6.) 

**Q: What do I need to actually collect my share?** Family Registration Certificate from NADRA listing all heirs, then either a succession certificate (movable assets — via NADRA or civil court) or a mutation at the revenue office/patwari (immovable property/land). **Q: My husband wants to marry a second wife. **Q: If I get khula, do I lose my Haq Mehr?** Depends on why the marriage is ending.
