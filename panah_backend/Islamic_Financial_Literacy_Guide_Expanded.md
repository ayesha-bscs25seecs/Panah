## 1\. Hiba (Gifts) vs. Inheritance

**In plain words:** A Hiba is something you give away completely, right now, while you're alive. Once you hand it over and the other person accepts it, it's theirs — no take-backs, and it has nothing to do with the fixed inheritance shares that apply after death.

A Hiba is something given away while the giver is still alive — once it's properly handed over, it belongs completely to the receiver from that moment on.

**Hadith:**

The Prophet ﷺ said, as narrated by Zaid bin Thabit:

_"Whoever gives a life-long gift, it belongs to the one to whom he gave it, both during his life and after his death. And do not give things on the basis of Ruqba, for whoever is given something on the basis of Ruqba, it becomes part of his estate."_ — Sunan an-Nasa'i 3723, Sahih

Inheritance is completely different: it only takes effect after someone dies, and it follows fixed shares set by Islamic law, not personal choice.

**For a Hiba to count as valid, three things must happen:** 1. The giver declares the gift. 2. The receiver accepts it. 3. Actual possession is handed over.

A gift involves no monetary consideration — this distinguishes it from a sale or exchange. Once properly completed, a Hiba cannot be revoked, and the receiver cannot be forced to give anything back in exchange.

_Scenario: Fatima's father owns a small shop. While he's alive and in good health, he decides to gift the shop entirely to Fatima — he signs it over, hands her the keys, and she starts running it. This is a valid Hiba: the shop is now completely hers, and this gift does not reduce or replace whatever inheritance share she'll still be legally entitled to later, when her father passes away and the estate is divided among all his heirs._

### The Pakistan-Specific Problem to Know

This exact tool is sometimes misused. Research on Pakistani family law shows that **"Hiba Tafzili"** (preferential gifting) is often used as a tool to alienate women from their ancestral property, and Pakistani courts show inconsistent rulings on this practice — meaning some fathers gift away most or all property to sons while alive, specifically to leave daughters with little left to inherit later. Knowing the difference between a Hiba and inheritance helps a woman recognize when this is happening to her.

_Sources: Khaleej Times — "Islamic Inheritance: Hiba"; iPleaders — "Concept of Gift Under Islamic Law"; Al-Amir Law Journal (Pakistan) — research paper on Hiba Tafzili and women's inheritance rights._

## 2\. Islamic Will (Wasiyyah)

**In plain words:** A Wasiyyah is different from a Hiba because it only kicks in after you die — and unlike a gift, you can't will away everything. You're only allowed to redirect up to one-third of your estate through a will; the rest automatically goes to your legal heirs in their fixed Quranic shares, no matter what the will says.

A Muslim may give away their entire property during their lifetime through a gift, but only one-third of the total estate can be bequeathed through a will. The remaining two-thirds must automatically follow the fixed Quranic inheritance shares — a will cannot override those shares or redirect that portion to someone who is already a legal heir.

**Hadith:**

The Prophet ﷺ said:

_"You may bequeath one-third, and one-third is a lot."_

It was narrated from 'Aishah that the Messenger of Allah ﷺ came to visit Sa'd (when he was sick). Sa'd said to him: "O Messenger of Allah, shall I bequeath two-thirds of my wealth?" He said: "No." He said: "Shall I bequeath half?" He said: "No." He said: "Shall I bequeath one-third?" He said: _"Yes, one-third, and one-third is much or large. If you leave your heirs independent of means that is better than if you leave them poor, holding out their hands… It is not right for a Muslim who has anything to bequeath to spend two nights without having his will written down."_ — Sunan an-Nasa'i 3633, Sahih

_Scenario: Sara wants to leave something extra to a needy niece who wouldn't normally inherit under fixed Islamic shares (since a niece isn't a primary heir when Sara has children of her own). Sara can legally will up to one-third of her estate to that niece. But she cannot will away more than a third, and she cannot use the will to reduce her own daughter's guaranteed inheritance share or redirect it elsewhere — that portion is locked in by Islamic law regardless of what the will states._

_Sources: iPleaders — "Concept of Gift Under Islamic Law"; IslamWeb Fatwa Center — "Difference Between Will and Gift."_

## 3\. Waqf and Sadaqah Jariyah

**In plain words:** Sadaqah Jariyah just means "ongoing charity" — any good deed that keeps helping people and keeps earning you reward long after you've done it. A Waqf is a specific, formal way of doing this: you permanently lock up an asset (like land, a building, or money) for a charitable purpose. You can never sell it, take it back, or pass it on as inheritance — only the _benefit_ it produces (like water from a well, income from a shop, or use of a building) keeps flowing to people, forever.

Zakat is obligatory and calculated precisely, with a fixed percentage and a minimum threshold (Nisab) that must be met before it applies. Waqf and Sadaqah Jariyah are different: they are entirely voluntary, with no fixed amount and no Nisab — the giver decides both the amount and the cause.

**Sadaqah Jariyah** ("ongoing charity") is the broader concept: any voluntary act of charity that keeps generating reward for the giver even after they've stopped actively giving, because the benefit keeps flowing to others indefinitely.

**Waqf** (a religious endowment) is a specific, formal type of Sadaqah Jariyah where the capital asset itself is tied up forever. It can never be sold, inherited, or taken back; only its ongoing benefit or income continues to serve the cause. In Pakistan, women can register a waqf through the Sindh Waqf Board or similar provincial bodies, or through a trust deed. The key requirement is that the waqf must be made during one's lifetime and with full ownership of the asset.

A woman can also contribute to an _existing_ waqf — like a mosque or school — by making a donation used for its maintenance. That, too, counts as Sadaqah Jariyah, even without creating a new waqf herself.

**Hadith:**

The Prophet ﷺ said:

_"When a person dies, his deeds come to an end except for three: ongoing charity (sadaqah jariyah), knowledge from which benefit is derived, and a righteous child who prays for him."_

_Scenario: Zainab inherits a small plot of land. Instead of donating cash just once, she uses the land to help build a water well that an entire village relies on for years. Even long after she's gone, people continue drawing water from it, and she keeps earning reward for as long as it's in use. This is different from Zakat: it isn't obligatory, has no fixed percentage or Nisab, and she chooses the amount and cause entirely herself._

_Sources: Islamic Relief Worldwide — "Takaful (co-operative insurance)" overview, which explains the Waqf endowment fund model as background; Waqf and Sadaqah Jariyah are also widely and consistently taught across mainstream Islamic jurisprudence sources, rather than resting on one single modern citation._

## 4\. Halal Investment Basics

**In plain words:** Avoiding interest (riba) doesn't mean avoiding the stock market entirely — it means being picky about _which_ companies you put money into. Pakistan has a built-in system that pre-screens companies for you, so you don't have to research each one yourself.

Avoiding interest doesn't mean avoiding the stock market altogether — it means being selective about which companies to invest in. In Pakistan, this has been made fairly accessible through a dedicated Shariah-screening system.

The **KMI-30 Index** tracks the 30 most liquid Shariah-compliant companies listed on the Pakistan Stock Exchange (PSX), weighted by market capitalization with a 12% cap on any single company's weight. Beyond just those 30, Islamic scholars — including those on Meezan Bank's Shariah Supervisory Board — along with SECP's own published Shariah screening guidelines from August 2022, have formally ruled that investing in Shariah-compliant equities on PSX is permissible, since it means becoming a part-owner of a real, operating business rather than gambling.

_Scenario: Hina has Rs. 100,000 in savings and wants to invest instead of leaving it idle, but she doesn't want to research individual stocks or risk accidentally investing in an interest-based bank or a company selling alcohol. Instead of picking stocks herself, she buys units of the KSE Meezan Index Fund, a Shariah-compliant fund designed to track the KMI-30 by investing in the same companies in proportion to their weight in the index. This way her investment automatically stays within Shariah-screened companies without her needing to verify each one manually._

_Sources: Pakistan Stock Exchange (PSX) — "Shariah Compliant Investment" page; Al Meezan Group — KSE Meezan Index Fund page; Hawksglobal.pk — "Halal Stocks Pakistan Guide" (2026)._

## 5\. Takaful (Islamic Insurance)

**In plain words:** Regular insurance is problematic in Islam mainly because of interest and too much uncertainty in how premiums and payouts work. Takaful fixes this by turning it into a group effort: everyone puts money into a shared pool as a _donation_, and whoever in the group has a loss gets paid out from that same pool — nobody is betting against anybody else.

Conventional insurance is considered problematic in Islam mainly because of interest and excessive uncertainty in how premiums and payouts work. Takaful solves this by turning it into a cooperative pool instead of a profit-driven contract. Participants pay contributions in the form of donations to help others in need, and instead of passing risk to an insurer, they share the risk together.

Each participant contributes to a common fund — the **takaful pool** — and the amount paid is treated as a donation (**tabarru'**), not a premium. In the **wakala waqf model**, part of the contribution goes to a separate waqf fund, which adds an extra layer of charity on top of the risk-sharing itself.

In Pakistan specifically, the Securities and Exchange Commission of Pakistan (SECP) has supported the industry's growth, and the **Takaful Rules of 2012** allowed conventional insurers to launch dedicated Takaful windows. The dominant structure used in Pakistan is the **"wakala waqf"** model.

_Scenario: Amina runs a small home-based business and wants to protect her equipment against fire or theft, but doesn't want to use a conventional insurance policy because of the interest and uncertainty involved. She takes a Takaful plan instead: she contributes a set amount into a shared pool with other participants. If her equipment is damaged, she's compensated from that pool. If no one in the pool makes a claim that year, any leftover surplus is either shared back among participants or reinvested for their benefit — unlike conventional insurance, where leftover surplus simply becomes company profit._

_Sources: SmartBenefits.pk — "Understanding Takaful: The Emerging Role of Islamic Insurance in Pakistan"; Habib Insurance — "How Takaful Works & Why You Should Consider It"; The Actuary (UK) — "Takaful Models of Islamic Insurance."_

## 6\. Guardianship of Children's Finances

**In plain words:** Raising a child day-to-day (custody) and having the legal authority to manage a child's money or property (guardianship) are two separate things in Pakistani law. A mother almost always gets the first one automatically. The second one — especially the power to sell or transfer a child's property — usually requires a separate court appointment, even for the mother.

This matters most for widowed or divorced women. In Pakistan, custody (day-to-day care) and guardianship (legal authority over decisions and property) are two separate things. Custody is the bringing up, nursing, and fostering of the child on a day-to-day basis, whereas guardianship means the power to carry out legal transactions and contracts with responsibility for the legal consequences.

Traditionally, the father is treated as the natural guardian, while the mother has priority for custody of boys until around age 7 and girls until puberty under Hanafi law, subject to the child's overall welfare. **The important financial detail:** a mother can be the de facto guardian of a minor's property, but she has no power to actually transfer or sell it. Alienating a minor's immovable property is only possible by the person legally entitled to be appointed property guardian, after obtaining the court's permission.

_Scenario: Sadia is widowed, and her late husband's life insurance payout and some property are meant for their two young children. She has automatic day-to-day custody and can raise them normally, but if she ever needs to sell an inherited plot of land that legally belongs to her children — say, to fund their education — she cannot do this on her own signature alone. Under the Guardian and Wards Act 1890, she would generally need to formally apply to the family court to be recognized as their legal property guardian before she can transact on their behalf. Knowing this in advance prevents delays or a transaction being challenged later as invalid._

_Sources: SAHSOL (Shaikh Ahmad Hassan School of Law, LUMS) — "Law on the Custody of Children in Pakistan"; CourtingTheLaw.com — "Law of Appointment of Guardian Under the Guardian & Wards Act 1890"; SJ Law Experts — "Guardianship Under Islamic Law in Pakistan."_

## 7\. Quick Summary

- **Hiba (gift):** given while alive, takes effect immediately, unlimited in amount — but should never be used to unfairly cut daughters out of their later inheritance (watch for "Hiba Tafzili" misuse).
- **Wasiyyah (will):** only takes effect after death, and can only redirect up to one-third of the estate to non-heirs.
- **Waqf / Sadaqah Jariyah:** voluntary, ongoing charity — no fixed amount or Nisab, chosen entirely by the giver. A Waqf specifically locks up the asset itself, forever.
- **Halal investing:** PSX's KMI-30 index and Shariah-compliant funds make it possible to invest without picking individual stocks yourself.
- **Takaful:** a cooperative, donation-based (tabarru') alternative to conventional insurance, regulated in Pakistan by SECP under the wakala waqf model.
- **Guardianship:** a mother has custody rights automatically, but needs court appointment as property guardian before she can sell or transfer a minor child's property.
