# Introduction and Scope

This corpus is organised into five pillars, each sourced independently so any single pillar can be cited on its own: primary Islamic texts (Qur'an and authenticated hadith), the codified statutory law of Pakistan, binding appellate case law, a short discussion of advanced classical jurisprudence, and a bank of high-frequency FAQs cross-referenced to the first four pillars. Every factual statement carries an inline citation to a verifiable source — an officially published statute, a reported judgment, a numbered hadith in a recognised collection (primarily Sahih al-Bukhari and Sahih Muslim, cross-checked against Sunan Abu Dawud and Sunan Ibn Majah), or a named, mainstream Qur'an translation.

A note on provincial variation: the Muslim Family Laws Ordinance 1961, the Dissolution of Muslim Marriages Act 1939, and the Family Courts Act 1964 apply across Pakistan, but since the 18th Constitutional Amendment, provinces administer family courts and periodically pass their own amendments (e.g. Punjab's amendments to Section 17-A on interim maintenance). Court practice, designated helplines, and administrative forms can therefore differ slightly by province. Where this corpus cites a specific provincial amendment or helpline, that scope is noted; otherwise assume the federal/original statutory text.

# Pillar I — Primary Islamic Texts (Qur'an & Hadith)

Marriage (nikah) is a civil contract in Islamic jurisprudence; divorce is permitted but disliked. Five verses and four hadith below carry the entire weight of Pakistani khula/nafaqa doctrine — everything else in this pillar was cut as duplicative of these.

## I.1 Qur'anic Verses

**The Waiting Period (Iddah):**

**Surah al-Baqarah 2:228 —** States the general principle underlying gender-balanced marital rights that the Supreme Court quoted in Khurshid Bibi: wives have rights corresponding to their obligations, according to what is recognised as fair (ma'ruf), though men have a degree (of responsibility) above them; the verse closes the passage that leads into the khula ruling in 2:229.

_[Divorced women must wait three monthly cycles ˹before they can re-marry˺. It is not lawful for them to conceal what Allah has created in their wombs, if they ˹truly˺ believe in Allah and the Last Day. And their husbands reserve the right to take them back within that period if they desire reconciliation. Women have rights similar to those of men equitably, although men have a degree ˹of responsibility˺ above them. And Allah is Almighty, All-Wise. (Al-Baqarah 2:228)](https://quran.com/al-baqarah?startingVerse=228)_

_Source: Qur'an 2:228, as quoted by Justice S. A. Rahman in Khurshid Bibi v. Muhammad Amin, PLD 1967 SC 97 (see Pillar III)._

**Surah al-Ahzab 33:49 —**

_[O believers! If you marry believing women and then divorce them before you touch them, they will have no waiting period for you to count, so give them a ˹suitable˺ compensation, and let them go graciously. (Al-Ahzab 33:49)](https://quran.com/al-ahzab?startingVerse=49)_

_Source: Qur'an 33:49; Quran.com_

**The Procedure and Ethics of Talaq:**

**Surah al-Talaq 65:1 —** Addressed to the Prophet ﷺ and the believers, this verse mandates the proper procedure for initiating divorce, requiring that it be pronounced at the start of a calculated waiting period (_iddah_) and forbidding husbands from evicting their wives from the marital residence during this timeframe. In Pakistani jurisprudence, this statutory "cooling-off" period and the prohibition against abrupt expulsion form the Quranic basis for Section 7 of the Muslim Family Laws Ordinance (MFLO), 1961, which enforces a mandatory 90-day period before a divorce becomes legally effective.

_[O Prophet! ˹Instruct the believers:˺ When you ˹intend to˺ divorce women, then divorce them with concern for their waiting period, and count it accurately. And fear Allah, your Lord. Do not force them out of their homes, nor should they leave—unless they commit a blatant misconduct. These are the limits set by Allah. And whoever transgresses Allah's limits has truly wronged his own soul. You never know, perhaps Allah will bring about a change ˹of heart˺ later. (At-Talaq 65:1)](https://quran.com/at-talaq?startingVerse=1)_

_Source: Qur'an 65:1; Quran.com_

**Surah al-Talaq 65:2 —** This verse governs the conclusion of the _iddah_, instructing husbands to either retain their wives honorably or separate with dignity (_ma'ruf_), while requiring two upright witnesses to formalize the decision. Pakistani family law draws upon this requirement of transparency and fairness; Section 7 of the MFLO reflects these principles by mandating formal notice to the Union Council Chairman and the appointment of an Arbitration Council to oversee attempts at honorable reconciliation or formalize a final, witnessed dissolution.

[Then when they have ˹almost˺ reached the end of their waiting period, either retain them honourably or separate from them honourably. And call two of your reliable men to witness ˹either way˺—and ˹let the witnesses˺ bear true testimony for ˹the sake of˺ Allah. This is enjoined on whoever has faith in Allah and the Last Day. And whoever is mindful of Allah, He will make a way out for them, (At-Talaq 65:2)](https://quran.com/at-talaq?startingVerse=2)

_Source: Qur'an 65:2; Quran.com_

**Prohibition of Taking Back Gifts (Mahr):**

**Surah an-Nisa 4:20 —** Directed at husbands who intend to replace one wife with another, this verse strictly prohibits demanding back or withholding any portion of the dower (_mahr_) given to her—even if it were a vast sum—characterizing such recovery as a severe transgression after the sacred covenant of marriage. Under Pakistani statutory and classical Islamic law, the wife's right to her full _mahr_ remains absolute in a husband-initiated divorce (_talaq_), and courts consistently enforce this verse to invalidate any coercive attempts by a husband to condition a _talaq_ on the surrender of her property or dower.

[If you desire to replace a wife with another and you have given the former ˹even˺ a stack of gold ˹as a dowry˺, do not take any of it back. Would you ˹still˺ take it unjustly and very sinfully? And how could you take it back after having enjoyed each other intimately and she has taken from you a firm commitment? (An-Nisa 4:20-21)](https://quran.com/4/20-21)

_Source: Qur'an 4:20-21; Quran.com_

**Consolatory Gift for Divorced Women**

**Surah al-Baqarah 2:241 —** Verse 2:236 similarly mentions compensation for divorces where no mahr was specified.

_Reasonable provisions must be made for divorced women—a duty on those mindful ˹of Allah˺._ _(A)_

_Source: Qur'an 2:241; Quran.com_

**Qur'anic Verses on Khula:**

**Surah al-Baqarah 2:229 —** This is the foundational verse on khula. It states that after two revocable pronouncements of divorce, a husband must either retain his wife honourably or release her with kindness, and that he may not take back anything he gave her as dower — except where both spouses fear they cannot maintain the limits set by Allah, in which case there is no blame on either of them in what the wife gives up (ransoms herself with) to obtain her release. This is the verse the Supreme Court of Pakistan relied on in Khurshid Bibi v. Muhammad Amin to found the judicial doctrine of khula (see Pillar III).

_[Divorce may be retracted twice, then the husband must retain ˹his wife˺ with honour or separate ˹from her˺ with grace. It is not lawful for husbands to take back anything of the dowry given to their wives, unless the couple fears not being able to keep within the limits of Allah. So if you fear they will not be able to keep within the limits of Allah, there is no blame if the wife compensates the husband to obtain divorce. These are the limits set by Allah, so do not transgress them. And whoever transgresses the limits of Allah, they are the ˹true˺ wrongdoers. (Al-Baqarah 2:229)](https://quran.com/al-baqarah?startingVerse=229)_

_Source: Qur'an 2:229 — rendered per Quran.com's Saheeh International, Ma'arif al-Qur'an (Mufti Muhammad Shafi), and Tafsir Ibn Kathir (quran.com/2/229 and quran.com/al-baqarah/229/tafsirs)._

**Wife's Right to Separation Due to Cruelty or Neglect**

**Surah an-Nisa 4:128**

_If a woman fears indifference or neglect from her husband, there is no blame on either of them if they seek ˹fair˺ settlement, which is best. Humans are ever inclined to selfishness. But if you are gracious and mindful ˹of Allah˺, surely Allah is All-Aware of what you do. (An-Nisa 4:128)_

_Source: Qur'an 4:128 — Quran.com_

**Surah an-Nisa 4:19 —** Addressed to husbands, this verse forbids inheriting women against their will or treating them harshly to force them to give up part of what has been given to them, except in the case of proven open lewdness (fahisha mubayyinah); it instructs husbands to live with their wives in kindness (ma'ruf), even if they dislike something about them, because Allah may place much good in what they dislike.

_[O believers! It is not permissible for you to inherit women against their will or mistreat them to make them return some of the dowry ˹as a ransom for divorce˺—unless they are found guilty of adultery. Treat them fairly. If you happen to dislike them, you may hate something which Allah turns into a great blessing. (An-Nisa 4:19)](https://quran.com/an-nisa?startingVerse=19)_

_Source: Qur'an 4:19 — Tafsir Ibn Kathir, cited in the discussion of khula-adjacent verses on Quran.com's tafsir pages for Surah an-Nisa._

**Surah an-Nisa 4:35 —** This verse prescribes the arbitration mechanism for marital discord: 'if you fear a split between them, send an arbitrator from his family and one from her family; if they wish reconciliation, Allah will cause it between them.' Pakistani statute law converts this Qur'anic arbitration principle directly into the mandatory Arbitration Council/reconciliation process under the Muslim Family Laws Ordinance 1961 and the Family Courts Act 1964 (see Pillar II).

_[If you anticipate a split between them, appoint a mediator from his family and another from hers. If they desire reconciliation, Allah will restore harmony between them. Surely Allah is All-Knowing, All-Aware. (An-Nisa 4:35)](https://quran.com/an-nisa?startingVerse=35)_

_Source: Qur'an 4:35, standard mainstream translation (Saheeh International / Quran.com)._

## I.2 Qur'anic Verses on Nafaqa (Maintenance)

**Surah al-Baqarah 2:233 —** Lays down that fathers/husbands are responsible for the food and clothing of mothers (during the nursing period) in a fair and reasonable manner (bi'l-ma'ruf), and that no one is charged with more than they can bear — establishing the principle that maintenance obligations are proportionate to the husband's means.

_[Your wives are like farmland for you, so approach them ˹consensually˺ as you please. And send forth something good for yourselves. Be mindful of Allah, and know that you will meet Him. And give good news to the believers. (Al-Baqarah 2:223)](https://quran.com/al-baqarah?startingVerse=223)_

_Source: Qur'an 2:233, standard mainstream translation._

**Surah an-Nisa 4:34 —** The primary verse establishing the husband's financial responsibility (qiwamah) for his wife, grounded partly in what husbands spend of their wealth on their families; classical and Pakistani jurists alike read this verse together with 2:233 and 65:7 as the Qur'anic basis of the nafaqa obligation.

_[Men are the caretakers of women, as men have been provisioned by Allah over women and tasked with supporting them financially. And righteous women are devoutly obedient and, when alone, protective of what Allah has entrusted them with. And if you sense ill-conduct from your women, advise them ˹first˺, ˹if they perist do not share their beds, ˹but if they still persist then discipline them ˹gently˺. But if they change their ways, do not be unjust to them. Surely Allah is Most High, All-Great. (An-Nisa 4:34)](https://quran.com/an-nisa?startingVerse=34)_

_Source: Qur'an 4:34, standard mainstream translation; discussed in Pakistani case law and commentary on maintenance (e.g., academic literature on the Muslim Family Laws Ordinance, 1961)._

**Surah at-Talaq 65:6 —** Directs that a divorced wife (during iddat) is to be housed according to the husband's means, that she should not be harmed so as to be forced out, and that if she is pregnant her expenses are to be paid until she delivers; if she nurses the child for the husband afterwards, he must pay her for it. This verse is the direct Qur'anic basis for iddat maintenance and, by extension, for child-rearing/nursing compensation claimed in Pakistani nafaqa suits.

_[Let them live where you live ˹during their waiting period˺, according to your means. And do not harass them to make their stay unbearable. If they are pregnant, then maintain them until they deliver. And if they nurse your child, compensate them, and consult together courteously. But if you fail to reach an agreement, then another woman will nurse ˹the child˺ for the father. (At-Talaq 65:6)](https://quran.com/at-talaq?startingVerse=6)_

_Source: Qur'an 65:6, standard mainstream translation (Saheeh International / Quran.com, Surah at-Talaq)._

**Surah at-Talaq 65:7 —** Instructs that a person of means should spend according to his means, and one whose provision is restricted should spend from what Allah has given him; Allah does not burden a soul beyond what He has given it — reinforcing the proportionality principle used by Pakistani Family Courts when fixing the quantum of maintenance.

_[Let the man of wealth provide according to his means. As for the one with limited resources, let him provide according to whatever Allah has given him. Allah does not require of any soul beyond what He has given it. After hardship, Allah will bring about ease. (At-Talaq 65:7)](https://quran.com/at-talaq?startingVerse=7)_

_Source: Qur'an 65:7, standard mainstream translation_

## I.3 Hadith on Divorce

- **The Most Disliked Permissible Act:**
  - **Narrated by Abdullah ibn Umar (RA):** The Messenger of Allah (PBUH) said: "The most hateful of permissible things to Allah is divorce."
  - _Source:_ Sunan Abu Dawud (Book 13, Hadith 2173), Sunan Ibn Majah (Book 10, Hadith 2018). _Graded Hasan or Sahih by scholars like Al-Albani._
- **Triple Talaq (Talaq-e-Biddat) was treated as ONE in the Prophet's time:**
  - **Narrated by Ibn Abbas (RA):** "During the lifetime of the Prophet (PBUH), Abu Bakr, and two years of the caliphate of Umar, a triple divorce was treated as a single divorce. Then Umar said: 'People have become hasty in a matter in which they were required to go slow. So let us make it binding on them.'"
  - _Source:_ Sahih Muslim (Book 9, Hadith 3491). _Note: This hadith proves that the Quranic ideal is a revocable, single divorce, but jurists later allowed the triple divorce to stand as final if pronounced, though it is considered sinful._
- **Divorce during Anger or Coercion:**
  - **Narrated by Aisha (RA):** The Prophet (PBUH) said: "There is no divorce and no manumission in the case of _Ighlaq_ (coercion/impaired mental state)."
  - _Source:_ Sunan Abu Dawud (Book 13, Hadith 2187), Sunan Ibn Majah (Book 10, Hadith 2046). _Graded Sahih._
- **Farewell Sermon on Women's Rights (Sahih Muslim 1218):**
  - In his Farewell Sermon, the Prophet (PBUH) instructed: "Fear Allah with regard to women..."
  - Another version states: "And indeed I order you to be good to the women…"
  - _Source: Sahih Muslim, Kitab al-Hajj, Hadith 1218; widely cited in Pakistani legal commentary as the broad prophetic mandate underlying statutory maintenance provisions._
- **The Husband's Authority and Responsibility:**
  - **Narrated by Ibn Umar (RA):** The Prophet (PBUH) said: "Each of you is a shepherd, and each of you is responsible for his flock... A man is a shepherd of his family and is responsible for them. A woman is a shepherd of her husband's house and is responsible for it."
  - _Source:_ Sahih al-Bukhari (Book 11, Hadith 853) and Sahih Muslim (Book 20, Hadith 4496).

## Hadith on Khula

- **The hadith of Habibah bint Sahl and Thabit ibn Qais (Sahih al-Bukhari 5273) —**
  - Ibn Abbas (RA) narrated that the wife of Thabit ibn Qais (RA) came to the Prophet (PBUH) and said, _"Regarding Thabit (RA), I do not fault his character nor his deen (religion), but I fear being ungrateful in Islam. I want to return his garden to him (in exchange for talaaq)."_  
    The Prophet (PBUH) asked, _"Do you really want to do this?"_  
    She replied, _"Yes."_  
    He then said to her husband, "_She wishes to return your garden to you (in exchange for talaaq)."_  
    The husband asked, _"Do I have the right to (accept it)?"_  
    The Prophet (PBUH) replied, _"Yes"_.  
    The husband said, _"Then I accept (the garden in exchange for talaaq)._"  
    **– (Musannaf Abdul-Razzaak vol.5 pg.391; HM Saeed**).
  - _Source: Sahih al-Bukhari, Book of Divorce (Kitab al-Talaq), Hadith 5273, chapter 'Al-Khul and how a divorce is given according to it' — sunnah.com/bukhari:5273._
- **The Muwatta / Sunan version of the same incident (Sunan Ibn Majah 2056; Muwatta Malik) —**
  - A parallel narration states that the Prophet went out for the dawn prayer and found Habibah bint Sahl waiting at his door; when asked what the matter was, she said she could no longer live with Thabit ibn Qais. When Thabit arrived, the Prophet told him she had said what Allah permitted her to say; Habibah confirmed she still held everything Thabit had given her, and the Prophet told Thabit to take it back from her, after which she returned to her family. Commentators (including Ibn Kathir) cite this narration when explaining the revelation-context (sabab al-nuzul) of Qur'an 2:229.
  - _Source: Sunan Ibn Majah 2056; Muwatta Malik, as reproduced in Tafsir Ibn Kathir on Qur'an 2:229 (quran.com/al-baqarah/229/tafsirs) and sunnah.com search results for 'Habibah Thabit ibn Qays'._
- **Hadith on divorce being the most disliked of permissible things (Sunan Abu Dawud) —**
  - Referenced in the Pakistani Supreme Court's own judgment in Khurshid Bibi, this hadith records the Prophet's statement that of all lawful things, divorce is the most detestable to Allah — a principle Pakistani courts cite to explain why reconciliation efforts are mandatory before any khula or talaq decree is finalised.
  - _Source: Sunan Abu Dawud, Book of Divorce, as quoted directly in the Supreme Court's judgment, Khurshid Bibi v. Muhammad Amin, PLD 1967 SC 97 (cite.pakcaselaw.com/PLD-SUPREME-COURT/1967/97/)._
- **Sahih Bukhari 5365**
  - The wording of this hadith does not mention "Women are the sisters of men." The recorded text is:
  - "The best women among the camel riders are the women of Quraish… the righteous among the women of Quraish are those who are most kind to their young ones and who are most careful about their husband's property."
- **Sunan Tirmidhi 1162**
  - This hadith specifically addresses the wife's rights:
  - The Prophet (ﷺ) said: "The most complete of believers in faith is the one with the best character, and the best of you are the best to their women."
  - (Note: This hadith is often quoted in the context of a husband's good treatment, not specific legal rights like maintenance or housing).

# Pillar II — Pakistani Statutory Laws

Sections below are summarised, not reproduced verbatim — only the operative rule and its practical consequence are retained.

## II.1 Muslim Family Laws Ordinance, 1961 (Ordinance VIII of 1961)

Applies to every Muslim citizen of Pakistan anywhere in the world; remains in force in all provinces post-18th Amendment (subject to provincial amendments, e.g. Punjab 2015).

**Section 7 — Talaq procedure**

After pronouncing talaq, the husband must give written notice to the Union Council Chairman and a copy to the wife. The Chairman must form an Arbitration Council within 30 days to attempt reconciliation. The divorce is not final until 90 days after the Chairman receives notice (extended if the wife is pregnant, until delivery). No intervening marriage (halala) is needed to remarry after a 1st or 2nd divorce.

**Section 8 — Other modes of dissolution**

The same notice-and-90-day procedure under Section 7 applies where the wife exercises a delegated right of divorce (talaq-e-tafweez) or the marriage is dissolved by any other means, including khula.

**Section 9 — Maintenance**

A wife denied adequate maintenance may complain to the Union Council Chairman, who forms an Arbitration Council to fix the amount; an aggrieved party may appeal to the Collector, whose decision is final and unappealable in any court. Arrears are recoverable as arrears of land revenue. In practice, Family Courts under the 1964 Act are now the dominant forum, but Section 9 remains valid law.

**Section 10 — Dower (Haq Mehr)**

If the nikahnama is silent on timing, the wife may demand the entire dower at any time. Prompt dower (mu'ajjal) is payable on demand; deferred dower (mu'wajjal) becomes payable on death or divorce.

## II.2 Dissolution of Muslim Marriages Act, 1939 (Act VIII of 1939)

Section 2 gives a Muslim wife the right to a court decree dissolving her marriage on any of the following fault-based grounds:

- Husband's whereabouts unknown for 4+ years
- Failure to provide maintenance for 2 years
- Unauthorised additional marriage in breach of MFLO requirements
- Imprisonment for 7+ years
- Failure to perform marital obligations for 3 years without valid reason
- Impotence at the time of marriage, continuing
- Insanity (2+ years), leprosy, or virulent venereal disease
- Option of puberty — married before age 16 by a guardian, repudiated before 18, marriage not consummated
- Lian — unproven public accusation of adultery by the husband
- Cruelty, including habitual assault, forcing an immoral life, disposing of her property, obstructing her religious practice, or unequal treatment of co-wives
- Any other ground recognised as valid under Islamic law

Special conditions: for the prison-term ground, the sentence must be final; for the missing-husband ground, the decree is suspended 6 months in case he reappears; for impotence, the husband may be given one year to disprove it. Section 5 preserves the wife's full dower entitlement regardless of the ground used.

## II.3 Family Courts Act, 1964 (West Pakistan Act XXXV of 1964)

Establishes Family Courts with exclusive jurisdiction (Schedule Part I) over khula, dissolution, dower, maintenance, restitution of conjugal rights, custody, guardianship, and dowry-article claims, plus limited criminal jurisdiction (Schedule Part II) between spouses.

**s. 3 —** At least one Family Court per district must be presided over by a woman judge.

**ss. 7–9 —** Suit begins by plaint; court issues summons within 3 days, defendant must appear within 30 days and file a written statement (which can itself carry a restitution or khula counter-claim).

**s. 10 —** Mandatory pre-trial reconciliation. If reconciliation fails in a divorce suit, the court must immediately pass the decree, and the wife returns any dower received (the operative khula mechanism, read with s. 10(4)).

**ss. 11–12 —** Evidence recorded informally; one final reconciliation attempt (max. 15 days) before judgment.

**s. 12A —** The entire suit, including divorce, must be decided within 6 months.

**s. 13 —** Unpaid decretal amounts recoverable as arrears of land revenue; instalment payment permitted.

**s. 14 —** Appeals lie to the High Court or District Court depending on the judge's rank. No appeal against a divorce decree (except for specified cruelty grounds), dower/dowry claims ≤ Rs. 30,000, or maintenance ≤ Rs. 1,000/month. Appeals must be decided within 4 months; filed within 30 days (Limitation Act 1908, Art. 152).

**s. 17 —** Strict Qanun-e-Shahadat and CPC evidence rules do not apply — the Family Court's procedure is simplified and faster.

**s. 17-A —** As amended 2015 ("shall", not "may"): the court must fix interim monthly maintenance on the husband's first appearance; if unpaid by the 14th of the month, his defence may be struck off and the suit decreed.

**s. 19 —** Flat court fee of Rs. 15 for any family suit or appeal.

**s. 25 —** Family Courts exercise full District Court powers under the Guardians and Wards Act, 1890 for custody.

**s. 25-B —** An appellate stay order automatically lapses after 30 days if not decided, preventing indefinite delay.

## II.4 Related Statutes Frequently Invoked Alongside Khula/Nafaqa Suits

**Guardians and Wards Act, 1890 —** Governs child custody and guardianship; invoked alongside khula/maintenance suits.

**Muslim Personal Law (Shariat) Application Act, 1937 —** Foundational statute applying Islamic law (rather than customary law) to Muslims in marriage and family matters; the DMMA 1939 supplements it.

**Dowry and Bridal Gifts (Restriction) Act, 1976 —** Protects a wife's dowry (jahez) and bridal gifts as her own property, regardless of how the marriage ends.

**Enforcement of Shari'ah Act, 1991, s. 4 —** Basis for Federal Shariat Court review of family-law statutes (used in Saleem Ahmad v. GoP).

**Family Courts (Amendment) Bill, 2024 —** Pending Senate legislation (sub-committee approved September 2024) that would require courts to fix a fixed monthly maintenance amount at the very first hearing. Not yet enacted law.

## II.5 Section 6 — Polygamy

A man may not contract another marriage during the subsistence of an existing one without the prior written permission of the Arbitration Council. He must apply to the Union Council Chairman stating his reasons and whether his existing wife/wives have consented; the Arbitration Council, after hearing both sides, grants permission only if satisfied the proposed marriage is necessary and just. The subsequent marriage itself remains legally valid even if contracted without permission — the Ordinance is regulatory, not prohibitory — but a husband who marries without permission must immediately pay the entire dower (prompt and deferred) owed to his existing wife or wives, recoverable as arrears of land revenue, and is separately liable on conviction to simple imprisonment up to one year, a fine up to Rs. 5,000, or both. An unauthorised additional marriage is also, independently, one of the specific grounds a wife may rely on to seek dissolution under DMMA s. 2(ia). The Federal Shariat Court upheld Section 6 as consistent with Islamic law in Allah Rakha v. Federation of Pakistan, PLD 2000 FSC 1.

**Legal / textual basis:** _Muslim Family Laws Ordinance 1961, s. 6; Dissolution of Muslim Marriages Act 1939, s. 2(ia); Allah Rakha v. Federation of Pakistan, PLD 2000 FSC 1_

## II.6 Provincial Variations and Local Amendments

Since the 18th Constitutional Amendment, provinces administer family courts and periodically pass their own amendments or standalone protective legislation. The federal statutes above remain the baseline everywhere.

**Islamabad Capital Territory (ICT)**

Cases are filed before the Family Courts in Islamabad under FCA s. 10; the specific court depends on the applicant's residence. A 2019 National Assembly committee bill sought to amend s. 10 for the ICT specifically, to involve family members in reconciliation — its status should be verified before reliance.

**Khyber Pakhtunkhwa (KP)**

The Family Courts Act, 1964 applies, with a 2020 amendment reinforcing jurisdiction over custody and child-related disputes. Separate legislation (e.g. the Kalash Marriage Act Bill 2026) governs specific communities' annulment and separation rules.

**Punjab**

The Punjab Protection of Women Against Violence Act, 2016 provides civil protective remedies — Protection, Residence, and Monetary Orders through Women Protection Officers and dedicated courts — for domestic, psychological, and economic abuse, running alongside (not instead of) ordinary Family Court and criminal proceedings.

**Sindh**

The Sindh Domestic Violence (Prevention and Protection) Act, 2013 similarly establishes a Domestic Violence Commission and protective-order mechanism for women, children, and vulnerable persons.

**Balochistan**

The original Family Courts Act, 1964 applies without a widely publicised standalone amendment or dedicated women's helpline. Users should rely on federal helplines (Police Emergency 15, Ministry of Human Rights 1099) and local counsel.

# Pillar III — Binding Landmark Judicial Precedents

In Pakistan, the written laws that govern family matters—such as the Muslim Family Laws Ordinance, 1961 (MFLO 1961), the Dissolution of Muslim Marriages Act, 1939 (DMMA 1939), and the Family Courts Act, 1964—do not actually give a detailed definition of _khula_ (a woman's right to seek a divorce). Because of this, the rules around _khula_ are almost entirely "judge-made." The law was built step-by-step through a chain of important court decisions, with each new case relying on and improving the ones before it.

Here are the major court decisions that created the modern rights women have to _khula_ today:

**III.1 Balqis Fatima v. Najm-ul-Ikram Qureshi — PLD 1959 Lahore 566**

Decided by a Full Bench of the Lahore High Court, this was the first major Pakistani court decision to state that a Muslim wife can get a _khula_ through the courts even if her husband refuses to give his consent. The court ruled that if a marriage is completely broken down and forcing the couple to stay together means they cannot live within the "limits of Allah" (as mentioned in Qur'an 2:229), a wife has a right to divorce. This laid the essential groundwork for later Supreme Court decisions.

_(Sources: Balqis Fatima v. Najm-ul-Ikram Qureshi, PLD 1959 Lahore 566; cited in 'Judicial Khula: Theory and Practice' via researchgate.net, and later in the Khurshid Bibi case, PLD 1967 SC 97)._

**III.2 Khurshid Bibi v. Baboo Muhammad Amin — PLD 1967 SC 97**

This is the single most important legal authority on _khula_ in Pakistan. Decided by a five-judge bench of the Supreme Court (S. A. Rahman, Fazle-Akbar, Hamoodur Rahman, Muhammad Yaqub Ali and S. A. Mahmood, JJ.) on 12 October 1966 and officially published in 1967, the Court established several vital protections for women:

- **Sources of Law:** The Qur'an is the primary source of Islamic law. The court noted that Hanafi jurisprudence also accepts _hadith_ (sayings of the Prophet), _ijtihad_ (independent legal reasoning), and _ijma'_ (consensus) as secondary sources.
- **Equal Standing:** Under the Qur'an (specifically Qur'an 2:228), husbands and wives have rights and obligations toward each other that are broadly fair and reciprocal, even if they are not exactly identical.
- **No Husband's Consent Needed:** Based on the historical incident of Habibah bint Sahl and Thabit ibn Qais, as well as Qur'an 2:229, the court ruled that if a wife has developed a deep, incurable dislike (aversion) to her husband—meaning they can no longer live within the limits set by Allah—she has an absolute right to a _khula_ decree. She can get this even if her husband does not agree, as long as she is willing to return the financial benefit (usually her _mahr_ or dower) that he gave her.
- **No Need to Prove Abuse:** A wife does not need to prove cruelty or that her husband did something wrong to get a _khula_. If she simply makes a clear, genuine statement to the court that she hates her husband and cannot live with him within Allah's bounds, that alone is enough.
- **True to Islam:** The Supreme Court explained they were not inventing new rules, but rather bringing back a true Qur'anic and prophetic principle from classical Islamic teachings.

This powerful ruling eventually led the government to officially write _khula_ into the law under Section 10(4) of the Family Courts Act, 1964 (as amended in 2002).

_(Sources: Khurshid Bibi v. Baboo Muhammad Amin, PLD 1967 Supreme Court 97. Full judgment available at_ [_cite.pakcaselaw.com/PLD-SUPREME-COURT/1967/97/_](https://www.google.com/search?q=https%3A%2F%2Fcite.pakcaselaw.com%2FPLD-SUPREME-COURT%2F1967%2F97%2F)_. This case is widely discussed in Dr. Muhammad Munir's article 'Judicial Law-Making: An Analysis of Case Law on Khul' in Pakistan' published in the Islamabad Law Review, as well as in legal commentary on courtingthelaw.com and sahsol.lums.edu.pk)._

**III.3 Mukhtar Ahmed v. Ansa Naheed — PLD 2002 SC 273**

In this case, the Supreme Court clarified that a woman has two separate and distinct paths to end a marriage. Seeking a divorce because a husband did something wrong (fault-based grounds under the DMMA 1939) is entirely different from seeking a _khula_ simply because she cannot stand to live with him anymore (no-fault "incurable aversion"). A legal case filed under one law cannot just be magically swapped to the other, because the courts require different types of evidence for each. _(Sources: Mukhtar Ahmed v. Ansa Naheed, PLD 2002 SC 273; discussed in the article 'Khula and Dissolution of Marriage Are Two Separate and Distinct Rights', published in The Friday Times on 24 March 2024 via thefridaytimes.com)._

**III.4 Saleem Ahmad v. Government of Pakistan — PLD 2014 Federal Shariat Court 43**

The Federal Shariat Court (FSC), which has the power to check if laws match Islamic teachings, reviewed Section 10(4) of the Family Courts Act, 1964. This section allows Family Courts to quickly grant a _khula_ at the pre-trial stage—without needing a long, drawn-out trial to gather evidence—once it is clear the couple cannot reconcile. The FSC ruled:

- **Husband's Consent is Not Islamic Law:** Forcing a woman to get her husband's permission for a _khula_ is not supported by the Qur'an or Sunnah. The Court found no rule in Islamic texts that stops a judge (_qazi_) from granting a _khula_ without the husband agreeing.
- **Fast-Track Khula is Valid:** The fast summary procedure in Section 10(4) is perfectly in line with Islam. The Court stood by this even though most modern religious scholars (_muftis_) they surveyed claimed a husband's consent was needed. The Court chose to follow the original, primary Islamic texts rather than the consensus of those modern _fatwas_.
- **Avoiding "Holy Deadlock":** Echoing a famous _hadith_ from Abu Dawud that divorce is the most distasteful of permitted things, the Court ruled that allowing a _khula_ is still far better than trapping a couple in a miserable "holy deadlock."
- **Iddat and Notice Periods:** The Court noted that the strict 90-day waiting period (_iddat_) and notice rules under Section 7 of the MFLO could be questioned under Islamic law regarding exactly when the _iddat_ timer should start. However, they ruled that the basic system of sending a notice to the local Union Council Chairman does not violate Islam.

_(Sources: Saleem Ahmad v. Government of Pakistan, PLD 2014 Federal Shariat Court 43. Summarized in 'Background and Prior Law' notes on academia.edu and course materials on the Islamization of Pakistani law on slideshare.net. It was also positively cited in PLR 2025 Lahore 3 = PLD 2025 Lahore 18 via pakistankanoon.com)._

**III.5 Later High Court Affirmations**

Several more recent High Court cases have continued to protect these rights:

- **PLR 2025 Lahore 3 = PLD 2025 Lahore 18 (Lahore High Court):** Reconfirmed that _khula_ does not violate Islam, and that the fast-track _khula_ process under Section 10(4) of the Family Courts Act, 1964 is completely valid, specifically relying on the _Khurshid Bibi_ and _Saleem Ahmad_ cases.
- **Ameer Bakhsh v. Additional Sessions Judge (Lahore High Court, 24 December 2021):** Applying the _Khurshid Bibi_ standard, the court confirmed that a woman can get a _khula_ simply because she does not want to stay married. Crucially, the court ruled that if a woman uses her legal right to get a _khula_ and later gets married again, her angry ex-husband cannot use the criminal justice system to prosecute her for it.
- **Muhammad Rafi v. Atta Ullah Kausar, 1993 CLC 1364** and **Syed Dilshad Ahmed v. Mst. Serwat, PLD 1990 Karachi 239:** These were important early High Court decisions made after _Khurshid Bibi_ that successfully applied and smoothed out the rule that a woman only needs to show an "incurable aversion" to get a _khula_.

_Source: PLR 2025 Lahore 3 (pakistankanoon.com); Ameer Bakhsh v. Additional Sessions Judge (durhamasianlawjournal.com case note, 2 May 2022); Muhammad Rafi and Syed Dilshad Ahmed cases as cited in sahsol.lums.edu.pk, 'A Woman's Right to Unilateral Divorce under Islamic Law'._

# Pillar IV — Advanced Islamic Jurisprudence

Four points of classical fiqh materially affect a woman's rights and are frequently misunderstood — everything else from classical jurisprudence not directly bearing on Pakistani practice has been cut.

**1\. Types of Talaq — Quick Cross-Reference**

The classification of Talaq-e-Raj'i (revocable), Talaq-e-Ba'in (irrevocable), and Talaq-e-Mughallazah/Talaq-e-Biddat (triple divorce) is defined in the glossary (Section 1: Divorce & Marriage Terms, above). The statutory point below is repeated here in full because it materially changes a woman's rights and is often misunderstood:

**Talaq-e-Biddat is capped at one revocable divorce in Pakistan**

The Supreme Court has effectively abolished the immediate triple-divorce effect: pronouncing "talaq" three times in one breath counts as one single revocable divorce under MFLO Section 7.

**2\. Khula vs. Faskh**

Khula: the wife initiates because she simply cannot live with the husband, and returns her mahr (or part of it) as consideration (Qur'an 2:229). Faskh/Tafreeq: the wife asks the court to annul the marriage because the husband is at fault (cruelty, desertion, non-maintenance, impotence, disease) — she does NOT have to return the mahr, because the husband breached the contract.

**3\. Mahr on divorce**

Full mahr is due if the marriage was consummated (Qur'an 4:20–21); half the mahr is due if divorce occurs before consummation, unless waived (Qur'an 2:237).

**4\. Mata'a (consolatory gift)**

Qur'an 2:241 requires a parting provision for divorced women — separate from mahr and iddat maintenance. This is a live reform debate in Pakistan: the classical Hanafi position generally applied here does not extend maintenance beyond iddat for the ex-wife herself (children's maintenance is separate and continues), though other Muslim-majority jurisdictions have expanded mata'a-based post-divorce compensation.

_Illustrative hadith — Fatima bint Qais (Sahih Muslim, Book 9, Hadith 3512): after an irrevocable triple divorce, the Prophet (PBUH) ruled she was not entitled to maintenance or housing, since she was not pregnant. This distinguishes a revocable divorce (housing/maintenance continues, since the husband may take her back) from a final one (only continues if she is pregnant)._

## IV.1 Understanding Khula

**FAQ 1: What is khula under Pakistani law, and how do I file for it?**

Khula (خلع) is a judicial divorce in which a wife asks the Family Court to dissolve her marriage without her husband's agreement and without proving fault or cruelty. She states she cannot continue the marriage within the limits prescribed by Allah, and offers to return her dower as consideration. It is granted under Section 10(4) of the Family Courts Act, 1964, following Khurshid Bibi.

To file: submit a plaint (with the checklist above) in the Family Court where the marriage was solemnised, where the spouses last resided together, or where you currently reside — this last option favours the wife. The court fee is a flat Rs. 15. The court issues summons within 3 days; the husband must appear within 30 days and file a written statement. At the pre-trial stage the court must attempt reconciliation (s. 10); if reconciliation fails, the court immediately grants khula, conditioned on you offering to return the dower received — no full trial is required. The whole suit, including any maintenance or dower claims filed alongside it, must be decided within 6 months (s. 12A). A khula decree cannot be appealed on the substantive question of dissolution, though procedural defects can be challenged in the High Court, and any appellate stay automatically lapses after 30 days (s. 25-B).

**Legal / textual basis:** _Qur'an 2:229; Sahih al-Bukhari 5273; Family Courts Act 1964, ss. 10, 10(4), 12A, 19, 25-B; Khurshid Bibi v. Muhammad Amin, PLD 1967 SC 97_

_Tags: khula, definition, filing procedure, how to file_

**FAQ 2: Does a husband's consent matter, and does the wife have to prove anything?**

No consent is required and no fault needs to be proven. Since Khurshid Bibi (PLD 1967 SC 97), courts have consistently granted khula once satisfied the wife has a genuine and incurable aversion such that the marriage cannot continue within Allah's limits — a firm, credible statement of aversion (usually on oath) is enough in itself. The Federal Shariat Court reaffirmed this in Saleem Ahmad v. Government of Pakistan (PLD 2014 FSC 43), holding that a husband's consent is not mandated by the Qur'an and Sunnah, even though most contemporary muftis surveyed by the Court disagreed.

**Legal / textual basis:** _Khurshid Bibi v. Muhammad Amin, PLD 1967 SC 97; Saleem Ahmad v. Government of Pakistan, PLD 2014 FSC 43_

_Tags: khula, consent, burden of proof_

**FAQ 3: What is the difference between khula, talaq, and faskh (DMMA dissolution)?**

Talaq: husband-initiated; he pronounces talaq and gives written notice to the Union Council (MFLO s. 7); effective 90 days later. Khula: wife-initiated; no fault required; she returns her dower; granted under FCA s. 10(4). Faskh (DMMA 1939, s. 2): wife must prove a specific fault ground (e.g. non-maintenance for 2 years, cruelty, impotence); she does NOT have to return her mahr, since the husband breached the contract. Per Mukhtar Ahmed v. Ansa Naheed (PLD 2002 SC 273), khula and faskh are legally independent — a suit under one cannot simply be converted into the other without amending the plaint.

**Legal / textual basis:** _Muslim Family Laws Ordinance 1961, s. 7; Dissolution of Muslim Marriages Act 1939, s. 2; Family Courts Act 1964, s. 10(4); Mukhtar Ahmed v. Ansa Naheed, PLD 2002 SC 273_

_Tags: khula, talaq, faskh, comparison_

**FAQ 4: Does a wife lose her dower if she takes khula? Can the amount ever exceed what she received?**

Ordinarily, yes, for the prompt/unpaid portion tied to the khula settlement — she is expected to return the dower or benefit received, per Qur'an 2:229 and the Habibah bint Sahl hadith. However, courts do not require her to return more than what she actually received; the classical position (traceable to Habibah returning only the orchard she was given, nothing more) limits the return to restoring the original consideration, not open-ended compensation. Unrelated claims — accrued maintenance arrears, dowry (jahez) articles, or her personal property — are not affected and remain independently recoverable.

**Legal / textual basis:** _Qur'an 2:229; Sahih al-Bukhari 5273; Muslim Family Laws Ordinance 1961, s. 10_

_Tags: khula, dower, compensation limit_

**FAQ 5: Can a couple remarry each other after khula, and is halala required?**

Yes, and no halala is required. Khula is treated, in mainstream Sunni practice in Pakistan, as equivalent to a single irrevocable (bain) divorce. The parties may remarry by a fresh nikah with mutual consent and a new dower — unlike after a third talaq, which does require an intervening marriage before the original spouses can remarry.

**Legal / textual basis:** _Muslim Family Laws Ordinance 1961, s. 7(6) (applied by analogy); classical Hanafi commentary on khula as one bain talaq_

_Tags: khula, remarriage, halala_

## IV.2 Nafaqa (Maintenance) During Marriage

**FAQ 6: What is nafaqa, and is a husband still obligated if his wife has her own income?**

Nafaqa is the maintenance a husband owes his wife (and a father owes his children) — food, clothing, and reasonable shelter, assessed by his financial means. Its basis is Qur'an 2:233, 4:34, 65:6–7 and the Hind bint 'Utbah hadith (Sahih al-Bukhari 5364); in statute, MFLO s. 9.

Yes, the obligation is still owed even if the wife has her own income. It arises from the marriage contract itself under the qiwamah principle (Qur'an 4:34) and is not automatically reduced by the wife's independent wealth or employment, unless she has voluntarily undertaken to bear household expenses. The husband's means remain the primary variable a Family Court weighs.

**Legal / textual basis:** _Qur'an 2:233, 4:34, 65:6-7; Sahih al-Bukhari 5364; Muslim Family Laws Ordinance 1961, s. 9_

_Tags: nafaqa, definition, working wife_

**FAQ 7: What can a wife do if her husband refuses to pay maintenance, and how is the amount calculated?**

She can file a maintenance suit in the Family Court. On the husband's first appearance, the court must fix interim monthly maintenance under Section 17-A (mandatory since the 2015 amendment); if he fails to pay by the 14th of the month, his defence can be struck off and the suit decreed against him outright. There is no fixed statutory formula for the amount — courts assess the husband's income and capacity, the wife's reasonable needs and social status, and local cost of living, applying the Qur'anic 'proportion to means' principle (65:7) and the hadith standard of 'sufficient and reasonable' (bi'l-ma'ruf). Amounts are routinely revisited and increased for inflation or a rise in the husband's income.

**Legal / textual basis:** _Family Courts Act 1964, s. 17-A; Muslim Family Laws Ordinance 1961, s. 9; Qur'an 65:7; Sahih al-Bukhari 5364_

_Tags: nafaqa, non-payment, quantum, interim maintenance_

**FAQ 8: What if the husband hides his income or assets, and can a grandfather be made to pay for a child?**

Family Courts can draw an adverse inference against a husband who conceals income or assets or otherwise withholds the best evidence of his paying capacity — the court can fix maintenance based on his likely real income and revise it upward if concealment later comes to light. A wife does not need to obtain his bank statements herself; she can ask the court to summon his employment or financial records.

On a grandfather's liability: the primary duty rests on the father, but where he is poor, infirm, or incapable of earning, and the paternal grandfather is financially well-off, Family Courts have held the obligation to maintain the child can shift to the grandfather — a useful, often-overlooked route where the father is absent, unemployed, or has fled abroad.

**Legal / textual basis:** _Family Courts Act 1964, s. 5 & Schedule; Qanun-e-Shahadat Order 1984, Art. 129(g); 2024 CLS 17 = 2024 CLC 141 = 2023 LHC 5606_

_Tags: maintenance, hidden income, adverse inference, grandfather liability_

**FAQ 9: Can a wife who has left the marital home still claim maintenance?**

Generally, a wife who leaves without lawful cause (nashuz) may have her maintenance right suspended for that period, under classical doctrine as applied by Pakistani courts. But if she left due to cruelty, non-maintenance, or another reasonable cause, her right continues, and the same conduct may itself ground a dissolution suit under DMMA s. 2. Courts assess this fact-specifically.

**Legal / textual basis:** _Dissolution of Muslim Marriages Act 1939, s. 2(viii); Muslim Family Laws Ordinance 1961, s. 9_

_Tags: nafaqa, nashuz, separation_

## IV.3 Maintenance During Iddat and After Divorce

**FAQ 10: Is a wife entitled to maintenance during her iddat period, and does khula affect this?**

Yes — iddat maintenance is a distinct entitlement grounded directly in Qur'an 65:6, requiring she be housed according to the husband's means during the waiting period, continuing until delivery if pregnant. Family Courts award this as a matter of course upon any dissolution (talaq or khula), for the duration of iddat (typically 3 menstrual cycles, or until childbirth). Khula does not extinguish this right — what khula requires her to forgo is the dower/benefit given as consideration for the decree; iddat maintenance is assessed independently and separately, generally payable from the decree date through the end of iddat.

**Legal / textual basis:** _Qur'an 65:6; Qur'an 2:229; Family Courts Act 1964_

_Tags: nafaqa, iddat, khula interaction_

**FAQ 11: Is maintenance owed beyond iddat (post-iddat / mata'a), and what happens if she is pregnant at the time of divorce?**

Under the majority classical Hanafi position generally applied in Pakistan, the husband's maintenance obligation for the wife herself ends with iddat — there is ordinarily no open-ended post-iddat maintenance for an ex-wife (children's maintenance is separate and continues). This is a genuinely debated reform area, though not yet codified into Pakistani statute.

If she is pregnant at the time of khula or talaq, her iddat continues until delivery, and the husband remains liable to maintain her throughout the pregnancy (Qur'an 65:6). If she subsequently nurses the child, the same verse directs him to pay for the nursing, by mutual agreement or, failing that, as fixed by the court alongside child maintenance.

**Legal / textual basis:** _Classical Hanafi doctrine as applied in Pakistani practice; Qur'an 65:6_

_Tags: nafaqa, mata'a, post-iddat, pregnancy_

## IV.4 Maintenance of Children

**FAQ 12: Who maintains children after divorce, and until what age?**

The father remains legally responsible for his children's food, clothing, education, and reasonable medical expenses regardless of who has custody, and regardless of whether the divorce was by khula or talaq. Custody (hizanat, typically the mother for younger children) and financial maintenance (nafaqa, resting on the father) are treated as legally distinct issues.

Fathers generally must maintain sons until majority or self-support, and daughters until marriage, though courts retain discretion to extend maintenance where a child remains dependent (e.g. still in education). There is no single rigid statutory age-cutoff — the standard is need and dependency, assessed case by case.

**Legal / textual basis:** _Guardians and Wards Act 1890; Family Courts Act 1964; Qur'an 2:233, 65:7 (proportionality principle applied to children)_

_Tags: child maintenance, custody distinction, age limit_

## IV.5 Dower and Other Financial Rights on Divorce

**FAQ 13: What happens to unpaid dower after khula or talaq, and can a wife keep her dowry (jahez) and gifts?**

Prompt dower is payable on demand at any time; deferred dower becomes payable immediately on dissolution by death, talaq, or khula (MFLO s. 10). In khula specifically, courts treat the wife's return of dower already received as consideration for the decree, but any genuinely unpaid dower remains a separate debt she can still claim.

Yes — dowry articles and personal gifts clearly intended as the wife's property remain her exclusive property regardless of how the marriage ends. Family Courts routinely hear suits for recovery of dowry articles (or their value) as distinct from, and unaffected by, the khula or divorce itself; only the specific dower she offers to return as khula consideration is affected.

**Legal / textual basis:** _Muslim Family Laws Ordinance 1961, s. 10; Qur'an 2:229; Family Courts Act 1964, Schedule_

_Tags: dower, jahez, financial rights_

**FAQ 14: Can I file for khula or maintenance if my marriage was never registered, or if I've lost my Nikahnama?**

Yes. An unregistered or verbal marriage does not automatically bar a khula or maintenance suit — while the Nikahnama is the primary evidence, the court can be satisfied of a valid marriage through other evidence (witness testimony, photographs, correspondence). Gather whatever evidence is available. If you have lost your Nikahnama, request a certified duplicate from the Union Council where the marriage was registered.

**Legal / textual basis:** _Family Courts Act 1964, s. 23 (registered marriages cannot be questioned); general evidentiary principles_

_Tags: unregistered marriage, nikahnama, evidence_

## IV.6 Practical and Procedural Questions

**FAQ 15: Is reconciliation mandatory, how long does a khula case take, and can a husband stop it with a restitution suit?**

Reconciliation is mandatory before any talaq (MFLO s. 7) or khula (FCA s. 10) becomes final, reflecting the Qur'anic arbitration principle (4:35) and the hadith that divorce is the most detestable of permissible acts.

Because Section 10(4) allows the court to decree khula at the pre-trial stage without recording full evidence, khula suits are designed to move faster than fault-based DMMA suits or contested maintenance claims. In practice, delays still occur from non-appearance or stay applications, which is why Section 25-B caps stay orders at 30 days.

A husband may file a separate restitution-of-conjugal-rights suit (as the husband did in the original Khurshid Bibi litigation), but once the court is satisfied of the wife's genuine aversion, a pending or even successful restitution suit is not treated as an absolute bar to a khula decree.

**Legal / textual basis:** _Qur'an 4:35; Muslim Family Laws Ordinance 1961, s. 7; Family Courts Act 1964, ss. 10, 10(4), 25-B; Khurshid Bibi v. Muhammad Amin, PLD 1967 SC 97_

_Tags: reconciliation, timeline, restitution of conjugal rights_

**FAQ 16: Is khula recognised as genuinely Islamic, and does a wife need a lawyer?**

Yes — the Supreme Court in Khurshid Bibi grounded khula directly in Qur'an 2:229 and the Habibah bint Sahl hadith, and the Federal Shariat Court (the body specifically tasked with testing laws against Islamic injunctions) independently confirmed in Saleem Ahmad v. GoP that Section 10(4) is not repugnant to the Qur'an and Sunnah.

A lawyer is not a strict legal requirement, but given the procedural steps involved (drafting the plaint, serving summons, reconciliation appearances, interim maintenance applications), most petitioners engage one experienced in Family Court practice. Family Court suits are exempt from court fees beyond the flat Rs. 15, which reduces — but does not eliminate — the cost barrier.

**Legal / textual basis:** _Qur'an 2:229; Sahih al-Bukhari 5273; Khurshid Bibi v. Muhammad Amin, PLD 1967 SC 97; Saleem Ahmad v. Government of Pakistan, PLD 2014 FSC 43_

_Tags: khula legitimacy, legal representation_

**FAQ 17: What are the appeal deadlines, and how do I enforce a decree the husband won't pay?**

Appeals against Family Court decrees must be filed within 30 days of the decree (courts may condone delay for sufficient cause, but generally insist on compliance). If a husband fails to pay a maintenance or other monetary decree, the decree-holder may file an execution petition before the same Family Court; there is no rigid deadline in the Family Courts Act itself, but the petition must be filed within the period the Limitation Act, 1908 prescribes for that type of relief. There is likewise no specific statutory timeline for a High Court revision petition — general Civil Procedure Code principles (filing 'without unreasonable delay') apply, so confirm current practice with local counsel.

**Legal / textual basis:** _Family Courts Act 1964, ss. 13, 14; Limitation Act 1908, Art. 152; Civil Procedure Code 1908, s. 115_

_Tags: appeal, execution, enforcement, timeline_

**FAQ 18: Is there a proposed law to make maintenance faster or more certain?**

Yes — a Family Courts (Amendment) Bill, 2024 was introduced in the Senate (approved by a Senate sub-committee in September 2024) that would require courts to fix a fixed monthly maintenance amount for dependent divorced women and children at the very first hearing, rather than leaving it to be worked out later. As of this compilation it is a pending legislative proposal, not yet enacted, and does not bind any court — this entry should be updated once (or if) it is passed.

**Legal / textual basis:** _Family Courts (Amendment) Bill, 2024 (Senate, pending — not yet law)_

_Tags: pending legislation, reform, first hearing_

**FAQ 19: Questions covered:**

_"Can I get khula without telling my husband?"_

**Direct Legal Answer:** **No.** Under Pakistani law and Islamic jurisprudence, a wife cannot obtain a valid _Khula_ decree in total secrecy without informing or attempting to serve notice to her husband.

Pakistani civil and family law requires strict adherence to constitutional due process. A court cannot proceed with a divorce decree without making every statutory effort to notify the respondent (husband).

**Statutory Breakdown & Legal Procedures**

**1\. Mandatory Service of Summons**

Under **Section 8 of the West Pakistan Family Courts Act, 1964**, immediately upon the filing of a _Khula_ plaint, the Family Court is legally bound to issue summons to the husband using multiple verified channels:

- Direct service via court process server.
- Registered post with acknowledgment due (AD) and courier service.
- Substituted service via publication in daily national newspapers if direct service fails.

**2\. Mandatory Pre-Trial Reconciliation Hearing**

Under **Section 10 of the West Pakistan Family Courts Act, 1964**:

- The judge is statutorily required to hold a pre-trial reconciliation session between both spouses to explore whether the marriage can be saved.
- Although the court can grant _Khula_ even if the husband refuses to agree, he must be formally given the opportunity to appear before the court.

**3\. Ex-Parte Decrees vs. Secret Decrees**

If a husband deliberately evades court summons, hides, or refuses to attend despite newspaper notices:

- **Ex-Parte Proceedings (Section 9(5)):** The Family Court may issue an _ex-parte Khula decree_ in his absence. This is **not** a secret divorce—it means the legal notice was publicly executed, but the husband chose to default.
- **Fraudulent Concealment (Section 9(6)):** Suppressing the lawsuit, supplying a false address, or deliberately keeping the husband unaware to obtain a _Khula_ constitutes fraud upon the court. Such a decree is voidable and can be set aside upon application by the husband.

**Source(s):** _Ministry of law and Justice & Pakistan Lawyer.site_

**FAQ 20: Can I get khula if I don't know where my husband is?**

Yes, in principle. The Code of Civil Procedure, 1908, Order V (applied by the West Pakistan Family Courts Rules, 1965) provides for substituted service — including service by publication — where a defendant's whereabouts cannot be ascertained through ordinary means, after the court is satisfied normal service is not possible. This mirrors the logic already recognised in the Dissolution of Muslim Marriages Act, 1939, s. 2(i), which lets a wife seek dissolution once a husband's whereabouts have been unknown for four years, and s. 3, which sets out the procedure (including notice to the husband's heirs) for such suits.

**FAQ 21: Questions covered:**

**Can You File for Khula Without a Registered Nikahnama or Physical Document?**

**Direct Legal Answer:** **Yes.** Under Pakistani law and Islamic jurisprudence, the non-registration of a marriage or the loss of the physical _Nikahnama_ does **not** invalidate the marriage itself, nor does it bar a woman from seeking _Khula_ in a Family Court.

**Statutory Breakdown & Legal Procedures**

**1\. Registration vs. Validity of Marriage (_Nikah_)**

Under **Section 5 of the Muslim Family Laws Ordinance (MFLO), 1961**, every marriage solemnized under Muslim law must be registered with the Union Council. However:

- **Statutory Reality:** Failure to register a _Nikah_ is an offense punishable by a fine or imprisonment under Section 5(4) of MFLO 1961, but it **does not render the marriage void or invalid** under Shariah or Pakistani civil law.
- **Family Court Jurisdiction:** A Family Court can adjudicate a _Khula_ suit for an unregistered marriage once the subsistence of the _Nikah_ is established.

**2\. Proving Marriage Without a Physical _Nikahnama_**

Under the **Qanun-e-Shahadat Order, 1984 (Law of Evidence)**, if primary documentary evidence (the original _Nikahnama_) is unavailable or lost, secondary evidence is legally admissible to prove the marriage:

- **Witness Testimony:** Oral evidence from witnesses who were present at the _Nikah_ ceremony (_Ejab-o-Qubool_), including the _Nikah Khawan_ (officiant), _Vakil_, or witnesses (_Gawahs_).
- **Secondary Documentation:** Birth certificates of children (B-Forms), joint bank account records, photographs/videos of the ceremony, or official communications identifying each other as spouses.
- **Admission/Presumption of Marriage:** Continuous cohabitation as husband and wife creates a strong legal presumption of a valid marriage in Pakistani courts.

**3\. Procedural Path in Family Court**

When filing a suit for _Khula_ without a physical _Nikahnama_:

- The plaintiff (wife) attaches an **Affidavit of Marriage** along with the plaint under Section 7 of the _West Pakistan Family Courts Act, 1964_.
- The plaint includes a specific statement detailing the date, time, venue, and witnesses of the _Nikah_, explaining that the document was either lost, withheld by the husband, or never formally registered.

**FAQ 22: Can I get khula if I have no money for a lawyer?**

**Can You File for Khula Without Financial Resources for a Lawyer?**

**Direct Legal Answer:** **Yes.** Under Pakistani law, financial distress is not a legal bar to obtaining _Khula_. Family courts carry minimal statutory fees, and multiple state and bar-mandated mechanisms exist to provide free legal representation (_pro bono_ counsel) to indigent female litigants.

**Statutory Breakdown & Legal Pathways**

**1\. Statutory Exemption from Court Fees**

Under **Section 19 of the West Pakistan Family Courts Act, 1964**:

- **Exemption Rule:** Family court suits are exempt from ad-valorem (percentage-based) court fees.
- **Filing Cost:** The formal court fee stamp required to file a _Khula_ plaint is nominal (typically fixed at Rs. 15 to Rs. 100 depending on provincial court rules). Lack of money to pay court registry fees is therefore legally non-existent as a barrier.

**2\. Statutory Right to Pro Bono & State Legal Aid**

Under Pakistani statutory frameworks, women without financial means can access legal representation through three official channels:

- **Legal Aid and Justice Authority (LAJA) Act, 2020:** A federal statutory body established to provide state-funded free legal representation and financial assistance to poor and vulnerable women seeking access to justice in civil and family law matters.
- **District Legal Aid Committees (DLAC):** Operating at the District Headquarters level under Bar Councils and District Courts, these committees assign pro bono panel advocates to women seeking _Khula_, maintenance, or child custody who meet indigent criteria (e.g., low-income threshold).
- **Free Legal Aid Committees of Provincial Bar Councils:** Under the _Legal Practitioners and Bar Councils Act, 1973_, provincial bar councils (Punjab, Sindh, KPK, Balochistan, and Islamabad) maintain mandatory Free Legal Aid Committees to represent indigent female litigants in Family Courts without charge.

**3\. Self-Representation (_In Personam_)**

Under **Section 18 of the West Pakistan Family Courts Act, 1964**:

- Legal counsel is not strictly mandatory to file or argue a family suit.
- A female plaintiff has the statutory right to present her own plaint, submit her statement on oath, and receive a judicial decree directly from the Family Judge.

**Protection of Personal Items, Court Assistance, and Legal Confidentiality**

**Direct Legal Answer:** **You are legally protected across all four scenarios.**

Under Pakistani statutory law and criminal/civil jurisprudence, confiscating your personal identity documents or phone is illegal; you have the right to be accompanied by a support person or representative in court; and your consultations with a lawyer are strictly protected by statutory privilege and confidentiality.

**Statutory Breakdown & Legal Protections**

**1\. Confiscation of Phone or Personal Documents (CNIC, Passport)**

- **Property Ownership:** A CNIC, passport, educational degrees, and personal phone are individual personal property. Unlawfully withholding them constitutes **illegal confinement of property** and **theft/extortion** under the _Pakistan Penal Code, 1860_ (e.g., Section 379/383).
- **Domestic Violence Protection:** Under provincial domestic violence statutes (e.g., _Punjab Protection of Women Against Violence Act 2016_, _Sindh Domestic Violence Act 2013_, and _KPK Protection of Women Against Domestic Violence Act 2021_), economic abuse and controlling access to personal identity documents or communication devices are recognized forms of domestic violence. Courts can issue immediate **Protection Orders** compelling the return of documents.
- **Secondary Evidence Rule:** If a husband forcibly retains your original _Nikahnama_ or CNIC copy, you do not lose your right to file for _Khula_. Under Article 76 of the _Qanun-e-Shahadat Order, 1984_, the Family Court allows you to submit secondary evidence or request a court order directing the husband or Union Council to produce the records.

**2\. Fear of Attending Court Alone & Support Companions**

- **Right to an Accompanying Person:** Family Courts in Pakistan are open civil forums. You have the absolute right to be accompanied to court by a parent, sibling, relative, friend, or social worker for emotional and physical support.
- **Representation Through "Next Friend" or Attorney:** Under Order III of the _Code of Civil Procedure, 1908_ (read with the _West Pakistan Family Courts Act, 1964_), if you fear direct intimidation or harassment, you do not have to attend every routine hearing personally. Your appointed Advocate or a recognized agent ("Next Friend") can represent you in court for procedural dates.
- **Courtroom Security:** If there is a threat of physical harm or harassment from the husband or his family on court premises, your lawyer can file an application to the Presiding Family Judge or District Police Officer (DPO) for special security arrangements or police escort within the court complex.

**3\. Seeking Legal Help Confidentiality (Without Family Knowledge)**

- **Statutory Legal Professional Privilege:** Under **Articles 9, 10, 11, and 12 of the Qanun-e-Shahadat Order, 1984 (Law of Evidence)**, all communications between a client and their lawyer are strictly privileged and confidential.
- **Fiduciary Obligations:** An advocate is bound by the _Pakistan Bar Council Canons of Professional Conduct_ to maintain absolute client confidentiality. A lawyer cannot disclose your consultation, contact, or legal filings to your husband, parents, or family members without your explicit written authorization.
- **Independent Adult Status:** Under Pakistani civil law, any adult female (age 18+) of sound mind has the full legal capacity to sign a _Wakalatnama_ (power of attorney), engage a lawyer, and initiate a lawsuit independently, without needing the consent or knowledge of her family or guardians.

_"Can I get divorced if my husband forces me into something?" · "Do I need evidence of abuse?" · "What evidence can I use?" · "Can messages be used as evidence?" · "Can medical records be used?" · "What if I don't have evidence?"_

**Proof of Abuse, Evidentiary Standards, and Digital/Medical Evidence in Family Courts**

**Direct Legal Answer:** **You do not need overwhelming documentary evidence to obtain a divorce or Khula.**

Under Pakistani law, Family Courts are flexible tribunals that evaluate both physical violence and mental/emotional cruelty. While documentary, medical, and digital evidence significantly strengthen a fault-based claim (DMMA 1939), **oral testimony alone is legally sufficient** to grant a dissolution of marriage or _Khula_ if it convinces the judge.

**Statutory Breakdown & Legal Protections**

**1\. Coercion & Forced Conduct as Grounds for Divorce**

Under **Section 2(viii) of the Dissolution of Muslim Marriages Act (DMMA), 1939**:

- **Definition of Cruelty:** Cruelty is not limited to physical beatings. It includes habitual ill-treatment, coercion, forcing a wife into an immoral life, or making her life "miserable" through mental torment.
- **Supreme Court Precedent:** The Supreme Court of Pakistan (e.g., _Tayyeba Ambareen v. Shafqat Ali Kiyani, 2023 SCMR 246_) confirmed that subjecting a wife to degrading, humiliating, or neglectful treatment legally constitutes cruelty.

**2\. Admissibility of Electronic & Digital Evidence (Messages, Audio, Video)**

Under **Article 164 of the Qanun-e-Shahadat Order (QSO), 1984** read with the _Electronic Transactions Ordinance, 2002_:

- **Admissibility:** WhatsApp messages, SMS, emails, audio call recordings, and video footage are fully admissible as primary/secondary digital evidence in Family Courts.
- **Evidentiary Threshold:** To ensure the judge gives weight to digital evidence, the party presenting it must show:
  1. The screen captures or voice recordings are authentic and unedited.
  2. The phone number or account belongs to the husband.
  3. Transcripts or physical printouts are submitted alongside the digital files.

**3\. Role of Medical Records & Official Documentation**

- **Medico-Legal Certificates (MLC):** A hospital emergency report or Medico-Legal Certificate issued by a government hospital serves as authoritative physical proof of physical battery/assault.
- **Police Reports (FIR/Madad 15):** Police emergency call logs or a registered First Information Report (FIR) under the _Pakistan Penal Code_ serve as corroborative documentary evidence of domestic abuse.
- **Psychiatric Records:** Clinical notes or prescriptions from a certified psychologist/psychiatrist documenting post-traumatic stress, anxiety, or depression caused by marital abuse are admissible to prove **mental cruelty**.

**4\. What If You Have NO Documentary or Physical Evidence?**

- **Exemption from Strict Technical Evidence Rules:** Under **Section 17 of the West Pakistan Family Courts Act, 1964**, the strict, rigid provisions of the _Qanun-e-Shahadat Order, 1984_ do **not** apply to Family Courts. Family judges evaluate cases on the "balance of probabilities" rather than "proof beyond reasonable doubt."
- **Oral Testimony (_Ocular Evidence_):** The sworn statement of the wife, corroborated by oral statements from family members, neighbors, or colleagues who witnessed her distress or heard the altercations, is sufficient for a decree.
- **Safety Net (_Khula_ Fallback):** If a wife fails to meet the evidentiary burden to prove fault-based cruelty under DMMA 1939, the Supreme Court has ruled (e.g., _2015 SC 804_) that the Family Court **must automatically grant her a Khula decree** based on her firm statement that she cannot live with the husband.

**FAQ 28: Questions covered:**

_"What if my husband hides his income?"_

**Legal Remedies When a Husband Conceals Income in Maintenance (_Nafaqa_) Cases**

**Direct Legal Answer:** **The Family Court can still fix and enforce maintenance even if the husband hides his income.**

Under Pakistani statutory law and established civil jurisprudence, a husband cannot evade his financial obligations by concealing his earnings. Family Courts have broad judicial authority to order asset disclosures, draw adverse legal inferences, rely on lifestyle evidence, and issue execution orders against his assets or employment income.

**Statutory Breakdown & Legal Mechanisms**

**1\. Court Powers to Direct Income Disclosure & Discovery**

Under **Section 17-A of the West Pakistan Family Courts Act, 1964** (Fixation of Interim Maintenance) read with civil discovery principles:

- **Duty of Disclosure:** When determining interim and final maintenance, the Family Court directs the husband to file his written statement along with documentary proof of income (e.g., salary slips, tax returns, bank statements).
- **Power to Summon Records:** The court has the statutory authority to summon third-party records under its civil powers—such as issuing directions to tax authorities (FBR), banking institutions, or employers to verify his true financial status.

**2\. Principle of Adverse Inference (Concealment Backfire)**

Under **Article 129 (Illustration g) of the Qanun-e-Shahadat Order, 1984**:

- If a husband fails to produce his salary slips, tax records, or bank statements despite possessing them, the court draws an **adverse inference**.
- The judge presumes that if the evidence were produced, it would expose higher earnings than claimed. Consequently, the court will accept the reasonable income estimate put forward by the wife.

**3\. Circumstantial & Lifestyle Evidentiary Threshold**

If direct salary documents are hidden, Family Courts determine maintenance based on the **standard of living** maintained during the marriage (e.g., _2022 MLD 731_ / _2021 IHC 7_). The wife can submit:

- Utility bills, rent agreements, or residential area proof.
- Vehicle ownership records or international travel history.
- School fee receipts of children (which serve as a baseline for the family's financial capacity).

**4\. Enforcement and Penalties for Non-Payment**

Under **Section 13 and Section 14 of the West Pakistan Family Courts Act, 1964**:

- **Salary Attachment:** If the husband is employed, the Family Court can directly issue an order attaching his salary at the source (directing his employer to deduct the maintenance amount and deposit it with the court).
- **Property Attachment & Auction:** If he is self-employed or concealing bank accounts, the court can attach and auction his movable or immovable property to recover arrears.

**Imprisonment:** Failure to comply with a maintenance decree can lead to civil imprisonment of the husband.

No, a father's remarriage does not reduce or eliminate his legal duty to maintain his children from a previous marriage.

- **Absolute Obligation:** Under Islamic jurisprudence (_sharia_) and Pakistani family law precedent, the financial maintenance (_nafaqa_) of minor children remains an absolute obligation of the biological father.
- **Potential Effect on Capacity:** While a father's financial capacity can be evaluated if he petitions the Family Court to modify a decree, courts generally maintain that taking on new domestic responsibilities (a new spouse or additional children) does not entitle a father to shirk or lower the established maintenance standard owed to his existing children.

**Does the mother's remarriage affect child maintenance?**

No, the mother's remarriage has no legal impact on the father's obligation to pay child maintenance.

- **Independent Financial Duty:** Under Pakistani law, a mother's financial status or marital status—whether single, employed, or remarried—does not shift the duty of child support onto her or her new husband. The step-father has no legal obligation under Pakistani law to maintain his step-children.
- **Separation of Custody vs. Maintenance:** Litigants often confuse custody (_hizanat_) with maintenance (_nafaqa_). While a mother's remarriage historically raised questions regarding child custody under the Guardians and Wards Act 1890, the superior courts of Pakistan have repeatedly clarified that maintenance remains strictly the biological father's liability, regardless of where or with whom the child lives.

**FAQ 30: Questions covered:**

_"Can the father take the child out of the city?" · "Can the father take the child abroad?" · "Can the mother move to another city with the child?" · "Can the mother move abroad with the child?" · "What happens if one parent takes the child without permission?" · "What happens if the child is taken to another country?"_

**1\. "Can the father take the child out of the city or abroad?"**

- **Out of the city:** If the mother holds lawful physical custody (_Hizanat_), the father cannot permanently remove the child from her city without her consent or express permission from the Guardian Court. Temporary outings to another city during court-sanctioned extended visitation require court approval or mutual consent.
- **Abroad:** No. The father cannot take the child abroad without the custodial mother's consent or a specific, formal order from the Guardian Court. Courts usually require substantial security bonds (_Nishan-e-Zamanat_) or bank guarantees to ensure the child returns to Pakistan.

**2\. "Can the mother move to another city or move abroad with the child?"**

- **To another city:** A custodial mother can relocate within Pakistan if the move serves the child's welfare (e.g., better job or schooling). However, if the relocation unreasonably deprives the father of his visitation rights or sever his access, the father can challenge the move in the Guardian Court. The court will adjust visitation schedules or reassess custody based on the child's best interest.
- **Abroad:** A mother cannot permanently move abroad with the child without the father's consent or prior approval from the Guardian Court. Because the father remains the legal guardian (_Wali_), taking the child overseas permanently without court sanction can lead to her losing custody, as it cuts off the father's statutory access.

**3\. "What happens if one parent takes the child without permission?"**

- **Within Pakistan:** Unlawful removal or retention by one parent amounts to illegal detention. The aggrieved parent can immediately file a **Habeas Corpus petition under Section 491 of the Code of Criminal Procedure (CrPC)** before the High Court or Sessions Court for the swift recovery and production of the child. The taking parent may also face criminal charges under Section 361/363 of the Pakistan Penal Code (PPC) for kidnapping from lawful guardianship.

**4\. "What happens if the child is taken to another country?"**

- **International Abduction:** Pakistan is **not** a signatory to the _1980 Hague Convention on the Civil Aspects of International Child Abduction_. _However, Pakistan has signed bilateral judicial protocols with some countries (e.g., the UK-Pakistan Judicial Protocol on Child Abduction) that provide some mechanism for return. In practice, Pakistani courts rely on Article 199 of the Constitution (writ jurisdiction) to order the recovery of abducted children._
- **Mechanics:** Because international treaty mechanisms do not apply automatically, cross-border return depends on **Bilateral Judicial Protocols** (such as the _UK-Pakistan Judicial Protocol_) or direct **Writ Petitions (Article 199 of the Constitution)** filed in the High Courts of Pakistan. Pakistani courts will assume jurisdiction based on the child's presence in the country and determine whether the removal was illegal and contrary to the minor's welfare.

**FAQ 31: Questions covered:**

_"What if the father is abusive?" · "What if the mother is being abused?" · "What if the child is unsafe with one parent?" · "What if grandparents are raising the child?" · "Can grandparents get custody?" · "What happens if neither parent can care for the child?"_

**Q-Group 54: Abuse, Parental Unfitness, and Grandparent Custody**

Under Pakistani legal frameworks, issues regarding abusive behavior, parent unfitness, and non-parent custody are evaluated primarily under **Section 17 of the Guardians and Wards Act, 1890**. While traditional Islamic jurisprudence (_Hanafi fiqh_) outlines a default hierarchy of custody (_Hizanat_), courts place the "welfare of the minor" as the paramount consideration.

**1\. Domestic Violence, Abuse, and Child Safety**

- **"What if the father is abusive?" / "What if the child is unsafe with one parent?"**
  - **Loss of Custody / Unfitness:** If a parent (father or mother) is abusive, violent, or poses a physical or psychological risk to the child, courts treat this as a disqualification for custody. Under Section 17, the court assesses the character, capacity, and fitness of the parent.
  - **Evidentiary Threshold:** Allegations of domestic abuse or child mistreatment must be supported by evidence (e.g., medical reports, police reports/FIRs, or psychological evaluations). Superior courts have emphasized using professional psychiatric and child-welfare assessments to evaluate parental fitness in contentious cases.
  - **Supervised Visitation:** If a parent is denied custody due to safety concerns, the court may restrict their access entirely or order strictly supervised meetings within the Family Court premises.
- **"What if the mother is being abused?"**
  - **Protection Orders:** A mother experiencing domestic abuse can seek dynamic protection and interim relief under provincial anti-domestic violence statutes (e.g., _The Punjab Protection of Women against Violence Act 2016_ or the _Sindh Domestic Violence (Prevention and Protection) Act 2013_).
  - **Impact on Custody:** Evidence that the father created an abusive home environment is weighed heavily in favor of the mother when determining the child's moral and psychological welfare in Guardian Court proceedings.

**2\. Grandparent Custody and Alternate Guardianship**

- **"Can grandparents get custody?" / "What if grandparents are raising the child?"**
  - **Yes.** Grandparents (both maternal and paternal) have explicit standing to petition for custody or formal guardianship under Section 7 and Section 17 of the Guardians and Wards Act, 1890.
  - **Order of Priority (_Hizanat_):** In Islamic law as applied in Pakistan, if the mother becomes disqualified (e.g., due to unfitness or passing away), custody of female children and young male children traditionally passes next to female maternal relatives—primarily the **maternal grandmother**—before reverting to the paternal family.
  - **Natural Parent Preference:** High Court precedent dictates that courts prefer keeping children with natural parents over grandparents. A grandparent cannot displace a living, willing biological parent unless that parent is explicitly proven **unfit** by a court of law. Financial capacity or higher living standards alone are not legal grounds to strip a biological parent of custody in favor of grandparents.

**3\. Inability of Both Parents to Care for the Child**

- **"What happens if neither parent can care for the child?"**
  - **Appointment of Third-Party Guardian:** If both biological parents are deceased, incapacitated, or deemed legally unfit due to neglect, substance abuse, or imprisonment, the Guardian Court will appoint a statutory guardian under Section 7 of the Act.
  - **Priority Order:** The court considers applicants in order of proximity and suitability: 1. Maternal or paternal grandparents. 2. Adult siblings (brothers/sisters). 3. Maternal or paternal aunts and uncles.
  - **State Protection:** Under special legislation (such as the _Islamabad Capital Territory Child Protection Act, 2018_ or provincial equivalents), if no suitable relative is willing or able to step forward, the state through Child Protection Welfare Bureaus can intervene to place the minor in certified care or protection facilities.

**FAQ 32: Questions covered:**

_"Can my husband keep my CNIC/passport/documents?" · "What if my husband refuses to return my belongings?" · "What if I don't have receipts?"_

**Legal Remedies for Recovery of Identity Documents, Personal Belongings, and Dowry (_Jahez_)**

**1\. Confiscation of CNIC, Passport, or Identity Documents**

- **Ownership Status:** A national identity card (CNIC) or passport is a non-transferable state document issued exclusively to the named individual. It is not marital property, nor does it belong to the husband.
- **Domestic Violence Statutes (Economic & Psychological Abuse):** Withholding identity documents, banking cards, or personal papers constitutes illegal restraint and **economic/psychological abuse** under provincial domestic violence legislation:
  - **Punjab Protection of Women Against Violence Act, 2016 (Sections 2 & 7):** Defines economic abuse and limiting freedom of movement as actionable domestic violence, empowering Magistrate/Family Courts to issue immediate **Protection Orders** directing the husband to return personal belongings and refrain from harassment.
  - **Sindh Domestic Violence (Prevention and Protection) Act, 2013** & **KPK Domestic Violence Act, 2021:** Provide parallel statutory protection and court directions for the recovery of personal documents.
- **Criminal & Constitutional Remedies:**
  - Unlawfully seizing or retaining someone's official documents amounts to **wrongful confinement/restraint of property** under the Pakistan Penal Code (PPC).
  - The wife (or her advocate) can submit an application before the relevant Magistrate or file a **Writ Petition under Article 199 of the Constitution of Pakistan** for the issuance of directions to the local police to recover her CNIC, passport, and personal documents from the husband's physical possession.

**2\. Recovery of Personal Belongings and Dowry (_Saman-e-Jahez_)**

- **Exclusive Female Ownership:** Under Pakistani statutory law and Islamic jurisprudence, all dowry articles (_Jahez_), bridal gifts, jewelry, clothes, and personal effects brought by the wife or gifted to her at the time of marriage belong **exclusively to her**. The husband or in-laws have no legal right to retain or withhold them upon marital breakdown.
- **Statutory Jurisdiction:** Under **Section 5 read with the Schedule to the West Pakistan Family Courts Act, 1964**, Family Courts have exclusive jurisdiction over suits for the recovery of dowry articles and personal property.

**3\. Proving Property Claims Without Purchase Receipts**

- **Exemption from Technical Evidence Rules:** Under **Section 17 of the West Pakistan Family Courts Act, 1964**, the technical and rigid provisions of the **Qanun-e-Shahadat Order, 1984 (Law of Evidence)** do not strictly (_stricto sensu_) apply to Family Courts.
- **Lack of Receipts is NOT Fatal:** The Supreme Court of Pakistan has repeatedly ruled (e.g., _2017 SCMR 393_, _PLD 2018 SC 34_) that a wife's claim for dowry recovery cannot be dismissed simply because she lacks purchase receipts or invoices. In Pakistani society, families rarely preserve store receipts for traditional wedding items or old purchases over many years.
- **Acceptable Alternative Evidence:**
  1. **Schedule / List of Dowry Articles (_Jahez List_):** A detailed inventory attached to the plaint listing all items (furniture, appliances, clothes, gold) along with their estimated monetary value.
  2. **Oral Testimony (_Gawahs_):** Statements from parents, relatives, or guests present during the wedding who witnessed the delivery of items to the husband's house.
  3. **Photographs & Videography:** Marriage photos or video recordings showing dowry items, gold ornaments, or bridal wear.
  4. **Decree Option (Return or Cash Value):** Under standard family court practice, the court orders the husband to return the specific items. If he claims they are destroyed, missing, or damaged, the court orders him to pay the **monetary market value** declared in the wife's schedule.

**Section A: Polygamy Rights**

**Q: "What if my husband takes a second wife?"**

Under Section 6 of the Muslim Family Laws Ordinance, 1961, a husband must obtain written permission from the Arbitration Council before taking a second wife. Section 2(ii-a) of the Dissolution of Muslim Marriages Act, 1939 allows a wife to seek dissolution if her husband takes an additional wife in contravention of these provisions.

**Key Points:**

- The second marriage is **not automatically void** even without permission
- The husband is still criminally liable (fine/imprisonment) under Section 6(5)
- The wife can seek **dissolution** under DMMA s.2(ii-a)
- She remains entitled to her **full dower** regardless
- Unequal treatment of wives is also grounds for dissolution under Section 2(viii)

**Section B: Remarriage After Khula**

**Q: "Can I remarry after khula? Do I need halala?"**

**Direct Answer:** Yes, you can remarry after khula. **Halala is NOT required**.

**Key Points:**

- Khula is treated as a **single irrevocable (bain) divorce**
- The couple can remarry by **fresh nikah** with new dower
- **No intervening marriage (halala)** is required
- Halala is only required **after the third and final talaq** (not after khula)

**Q: "Can I reconcile with my husband during the khula case?"**

**Yes.** During the 90-day waiting period, the Union Council attempts reconciliation. If reconciliation succeeds, the wife can withdraw her khula case and return to her husband before the divorce certificate is issued.

**Timeline:**

| Stage                              | Can Reconcile?                    |
| ---------------------------------- | --------------------------------- |
| During khula case (before decree)  | ✅ Yes - can withdraw case        |
| After decree but before iddat ends | ✅ Yes - reconciliation possible  |
| After iddat/divorce certificate    | ✅ Yes - but requires fresh nikah |

**Section C: Inheritance Rights**

**Q: "Do I lose inheritance if I get khula?"**

**Answer:** Khula does not affect inheritance rights that have **already vested**.

**Key Points:**

- A wife retains inheritance she received **during the marriage**
- She is **not entitled** to inherit from her ex-husband after divorce (unless named in a will)
- **Children's inheritance rights are unaffected** by divorce
- If the husband dies during iddat, the wife **may inherit** (depending on interpretation)

**Section D: Talak-e-Tafweez (Delegated Divorce)**

**Q: "My Nikahnama says I have right to divorce. What does that mean?"**

Column 18 of the standard Nikahnama allows a husband to **delegate the right of divorce** to his wife (Talak-e-Tafweez). This is recognized as valid under Pakistani law.

**Key Points:**

- Must be **explicitly stated** in Column 18 of the Nikahnama
- The delegation can be **absolute or conditional**
- If delegated, the wife can pronounce talaq **without court involvement**
- The procedure follows **Section 8 of MFLO 1961** (notice to Union Council, 90-day waiting)
- The Federal Shariat Court has confirmed this is **Islamic** and a check on husband's cruelty

**Important:** Mere statement of "all Sharia rights" (Shary haqoqq hasil hy) does **not** constitute delegation. It must be explicit and unambiguous.

**Section E: Stepfather's Responsibilities**

**Q: "Does my new husband have to support my children?"**

**Answer:** No. Under Pakistani law, a stepfather has **no legal obligation** to maintain his stepchildren.

**Key Points:**

- The **biological father** remains primarily responsible
- The mother's remarriage **does not** shift this duty
- Stepfather has no legal liability for child maintenance
- Some legal scholars recommend reforming this, but the law remains unchanged

# Pillar VI — Custody, Polygamy & Protective Remedies

These matters arise constantly alongside khula and nafaqa suits but are governed by separate statutes. Kept short and cited directly, not folded into Pillar II, since they are distinct causes of action rather than restatements of the divorce/maintenance rules above.

## VI.1 Child Custody (Hizanat) and Guardianship (Wilayat)

The Guardians and Wards Act, 1890 is the governing statute; Section 17 makes the welfare of the minor the paramount and overriding consideration in every custody dispute — courts weigh health, education, emotional stability, and the child's own wishes, not a fixed rule. Hizanat (physical custody/day-to-day care) and wilayat (legal guardianship and financial responsibility, ordinarily the father's) are treated as distinct. Under classical Hanafi doctrine as applied by Pakistani courts, a mother has a presumptive right to hizanat of a son until around age 7 and a daughter until puberty — but this presumption is rebuttable, not absolute, and yields entirely to the welfare test. A mother does not lose custody merely because she obtained khula; her remarriage to someone outside the child's prohibited-degree relatives is a factor a court may weigh, not an automatic disqualification. Custody petitions before the Family Court are full suits (pleadings, evidence, witnesses), not summary applications, and typically run in parallel with — not as part of — a khula or maintenance suit.

**Legal / textual basis:** _Guardians and Wards Act 1890, ss. 17, 25; Family Courts Act 1964, s. 25, Schedule Part I(5)-(6)_

**FAQ 19: Until what age does a mother keep custody, and can she lose it?**

As a starting presumption under Hanafi hizanat doctrine, a mother typically retains custody of a son until about age 7 and a daughter until puberty. This is only a starting point, however — Section 17 of the Guardians and Wards Act makes the child's welfare the paramount and overriding test, so courts routinely extend custody beyond these ages, or depart from the presumption entirely, based on the child's actual best interests, stability, and wishes. A mother can lose custody if the court finds her conduct genuinely harmful to the child's welfare, but taking khula, by itself, is not such a ground.

**Legal / textual basis:** _Guardians and Wards Act 1890, s. 17; classical Hanafi hizanat doctrine as applied in Pakistani practice_

_Tags: custody, hizanat, age limit, welfare of minor_

## VI.2 Polygamy (Second Marriage) — Cross-Reference

See Pillar II.5 for the full Section 6 rule. In brief: a second marriage without Arbitration Council permission remains valid, but exposes the husband to immediate liability for the existing wife's entire dower plus criminal penalty (up to 1 year imprisonment, up to Rs. 5,000 fine, or both) — and separately gives the existing wife an independent ground to seek dissolution under DMMA s. 2(ia).

**FAQ 20: Can my husband legally take a second wife without telling me?**

Not lawfully. Before contracting another marriage, he must apply in writing to the Union Council Chairman, stating his reasons and whether his existing wife's consent has been obtained; an Arbitration Council then decides whether the marriage is necessary and just. If he marries without this permission, the marriage itself is not invalidated, but he must immediately pay you the full dower (prompt and deferred) owed to you, and he is separately liable to prosecution — up to one year's imprisonment, a fine of up to Rs. 5,000, or both, on your complaint. An unauthorised second marriage is also, independently, one of the specific grounds on which you may seek dissolution of your own marriage under DMMA s. 2(ia), without needing khula.

**Legal / textual basis:** _Muslim Family Laws Ordinance 1961, s. 6; Dissolution of Muslim Marriages Act 1939, s. 2(ia)_

_Tags: polygamy, second marriage, consent, penalty_

## VI.3 Domestic Violence — Protective Remedies Alongside Family Court Proceedings

These run in parallel with, not instead of, a khula or maintenance suit. The Family Courts Act's Schedule Part II already gives Family Courts limited criminal jurisdiction over specified Pakistan Penal Code offences between spouses (hurt, wrongful restraint, criminal force, insulting modesty — see Pillar II.3). Beyond that, two provinces have standalone protective statutes:

**Punjab Protection of Women Against Violence Act, 2016**

Lets a woman apply directly to a court (or through a Women Protection Officer) for an Interim, Protection, Residence, or Monetary Order — covering physical, psychological, and economic abuse, and stalking/cyber-harassment — while a criminal case (if any) proceeds separately.

**Sindh Domestic Violence (Prevention and Protection) Act, 2013**

Establishes a Domestic Violence Commission and a comparable protective-order mechanism for women, children, and other vulnerable persons in Sindh.

**Legal / textual basis:** _Punjab Protection of Women Against Violence Act 2016; Sindh Domestic Violence (Prevention and Protection) Act 2013; Family Courts Act 1964, Schedule Part II_

_Tags: domestic violence, protection order, Punjab, Sindh, criminal remedies_
